Sami had exactly one job that morning: sit quietly in the tote bag and look presentable. He lasted about four minutes.

The market was already in full chaos by the time the tote swung through the gate — a fiddle player sawing away near the entrance, a kid on a scooter weaving between crates of peaches, somebody's very opinionated goat tied to a post and hollering about it. Sami peeked over the tote's edge for one look, just one, and that was his first mistake.

"Careful," said the voice carrying him, "you're not a sample."

Too late. A gust of wind — market winds are a menace, everyone knows this — caught the tote's flap, and Sami went tumbling, end over crusty end, straight onto a folding table stacked with heirloom tomatoes. He landed, somewhat majestically, right in the middle of a hand-lettered sign that said FREE TASTE.

A very small child pointed. "Sandwich!"

"That's not—" the farmer started, then looked at Sami, golden and glistening and admittedly looking delicious next to the tomatoes, and gave up mid-sentence.

What followed was, by Sami's own later account, four of the most harrowing minutes of his life: rescued by a stranger's polite cough, nearly adopted by the opinionated goat (who lunged with real conviction), and finally scooped up — slightly bruised at one corner, one piece of lettuce hanging on for dear life — by the very voice that had promised him a quiet morning.

"You," she said, "are a menace."

Sami couldn't really argue. He'd been proofed for eighteen hours to develop a nice open crumb, not a taste for danger, and yet here he was, market-famous, tomato-adjacent, and — he had to admit — having the time of his life.

He rode home in the tote with considerably more supervision, one flap firmly clipped shut, replaying the whole business in his head. A little chaos, it turned out, was exactly what a long, patient ferment had been building toward all along.
