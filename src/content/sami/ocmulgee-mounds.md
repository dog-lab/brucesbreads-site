Bruce the Bald Baker had a bone to pick, and this time it didn't have anything to do with the Giants.

It started with a comment on the blog. Somebody calling themselves ProofingPatty87 had the nerve to write, "y'all didn't invent farming bread, this is Georgia, people were growing grain here before Georgia was Georgia," and Bruce, who has strong feelings about his adopted home state, read it four times before deciding somebody needed to go check. He didn't send Fari this time — she had her own errands — so he sent Sami, alone, with strict instructions: "Bring back proof. And don't get eaten."

Sami thought that second instruction was a joke. Sami was wrong.

He made the drive south to Macon in his Giants cap, guidebook on the passenger seat, and parked at a place he'd never heard of: Ocmulgee Mounds National Historical Park. The Trading Post ruins near the entrance set the tone early — people had been swapping goods on this exact riverbank for thousands of years, long before a colonial-era trading post ever went up on the same ground, which meant Sami's whole well-traveled-sandwich self-image took its first hit before he'd even reached the good stuff.

The Earth Lodge came next, and it earned some real respect: the floor inside — the actual original clay floor, carbon-dated to around 1015 CE — is a thousand years old, and you can still stand on it. Built as a council chamber, raised earth over a wooden frame, with a platform shaped like an eagle and molded clay seats built right into the ground. Sami noted, professionally, that an earth-covered room like this holds a rock-steady temperature no matter what's happening outside — which is just a proofing box with better landscaping — and moved on before he got too sentimental about it.

Then came the Great Temple Mound, fifty-five feet of hand-carried earth, basketload by basketload, no wheelbarrow, no crane, just generations of patience. Sami was two-thirds of the way up the modern staircase, admiring a recent magnetometer scan that had found a spiraling staircase built into the mound that nobody even knew was there, when a squirrel spotted him.

It was, by any reasonable definition, an extremely opinionated squirrel.

It came in low and fast, tail like a flag of war, and Sami — a sandwich, it bears repeating, with nowhere to run that wasn't also fifty-five feet of open staircase — did the only sensible thing: he ran anyway. Down the steps two at a time, guidebook flapping, Giants cap nearly airborne, one indignant "I am not a snack!" lost entirely on an animal that had clearly made up its mind. A family coming up the other way flattened themselves against the railing. A ranger, radio in hand, watched the whole thing with the weary calm of someone who has seen this exact chase before.

Sami made it to the bottom, out of breath and slightly askew, right as the squirrel got distracted by an acorn and abandoned the pursuit without so much as an apology.

"Rude," Sami said, to no one.

It was at Cornfield Mound, still catching his breath, that Sami got the news that actually mattered. This mound is named for what's buried under it — a ceremonial cornfield, preserved on purpose, where archaeologists found charred corncobs and the parallel furrow rows still holding their shape after a thousand years. Not just corn, either: corn, beans, squash, sunflowers, and tobacco, cultivated on purpose, by people who clearly knew what they were doing. Sami, a sandwich built entirely on the promise of wheat, read the interpretive sign twice and had to sit with it. Whoever farmed this field had been growing grain in Georgia for the better part of a millennium before his own family so much as preheated an oven.

"Well," he said. "That settles that."

At Funeral Mound he went quiet, the way you're supposed to. It's a burial mound, and it still holds the people it was built for, and a sandwich with a sense of humor knows exactly when not to use it.

He finished at the River Bluff, looking out over the same floodplain the Mississippian farmers once worked, and thought about how the people who built all of this hadn't gone anywhere, not really — the Muscogee (Creek) Nation is still connected to this land today, working with the park on its future, including nearly a thousand acres called the Ocmulgee Old Fields they reclaimed back in 2022. Whatever ProofingPatty87 thought they were starting, this wasn't a story about a civilization that vanished. It was a story about one that's still here — and, Sami noted for Bruce's benefit, possibly about to get its own National Park designation, if the bill currently inching through Congress ever clears committee.

He called Bruce from the parking lot, cap crooked, one ear still listening for squirrels.

"Well?" Bruce said. "Did we invent bread here first?"

"No," Sami said. "Corn did. About a thousand years before we showed up, and it didn't even need an oven."

Bruce was quiet for a second. "Bring home some cornmeal," he said. "I want to try something."

"Already in the car," Sami said. "Also, I was almost eaten by a squirrel."

"Welcome to Georgia," said Bruce.
