Sami was, by any reasonable standard, a very good sandwich. Two thick slices of sourdough, golden and a little tangy, held together a stack of things Sami was quietly proud of — but even a very good sandwich gets restless sitting in the same bread box every day. So when the picnic basket lid creaked open one warm Georgia morning and a voice said *"road trip,"* Sami's crust practically crackled with excitement.

The basket rode along in the back seat for what felt like a very long time, jostling past pine trees and marsh grass and a sign that read WELCOME TO JEKYLL ISLAND. Sami caught glimpses of the world through the wicker gaps — flashes of blue, then green, then something that looked like silver bones scattered across golden sand.

"That's Driftwood Beach," said the voice from the front seat. "Wait until you see it up close."

Up close turned out to be extraordinary. The basket lid opened onto a stretch of shoreline unlike anything Sami had imagined from inside a bread box. Massive, bleached tree skeletons lay tangled across the sand — oaks and pines, felled long ago by the slow, patient work of the tide, their bark long gone, their wood bare and silver-grey and smooth as bone. Some lay flat, half-buried, worn round by decades of waves. Others still reached upward, branches bare, like something between driftwood and sculpture, frozen mid-gesture toward the sky.

Sami had never seen anything so beautifully worn down. *This is what happens when you let the ocean have its way with you for a while,* Sami thought, feeling a little more adventurous already.

A sudden gust of wind nearly sent Sami tumbling out of the basket entirely, and for one heart-stopping moment, it looked like this particular sourdough sandwich's story might end face-down in the sand. But a steady hand caught the basket just in time, and Sami settled back down, only slightly rattled, one corner of crust now charmingly a bit askew.

"Careful," the voice laughed. "We didn't come all this way to lose you to a sand dune."

They found a smooth, sun-warmed log to sit on — one of the fallen giants, silvery and cool despite the sun — and the basket opened properly at last. Sami was lifted out into the salt air, and for a moment, everything went quiet except for the waves rolling in over the tangled wood and the gulls calling somewhere overhead.

It was, Sami had to admit, a very good place to be eaten.

But first — a little exploring. Propped carefully atop the basket lid, Sami got a sandwich's-eye view of the whole scene: the tide pulling back to reveal wet, mirror-bright sand; a ghost crab making a break for a nearby burrow; a heron standing very still in the shallows, apparently unbothered by the whole spectacle of driftwood and disbelief around it. Somewhere down the beach, someone was building a fort out of a fallen branch twice their height, laughing every time the wind tried to knock it down.

Sami thought about all the ordinary days back in the bread box — the pantry shelf, the kitchen counter, the same four walls — and decided that this, right here, was worth every jostling mile of the drive. Sourdough doesn't get many chances to see the ocean.

When the moment finally came, it came gently — savored bite by bite on that smooth, driftwood log, with the tide rolling in and the whole beach glowing gold in the late afternoon light. Sami didn't mind one bit. Some sandwiches are made to be eaten quickly at a desk. Sami had been made, it turned out, for driftwood and salt air and a very good ending to a very good trip.
