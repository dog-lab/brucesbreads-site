Sami had been proofing all night in a cool corner of the kitchen, and by morning he was feeling exactly the way a good sourdough ought to feel: bubbly, a little bit stretchy, and ready for an adventure. His crust had that deep amber color that only comes from a long, slow bake, and tucked inside him was a whole world of good things — turkey, bacon, sharp cheese, and a crisp layer of lettuce and tomato that shifted pleasantly with every step. He'd packed a little guidebook titled *Tallulah Gorge State Park*, strapped on a backpack with a water bottle in the side pocket, and slung a small camera around his neck, because a sandwich only gets to see a thousand-foot canyon for the first time once.

## The Overlook

Sami's first stop was the rim trail, where the ground simply fell away into an enormous green-and-granite canyon, the Tallulah River glinting far below. Two waterfalls announced themselves from here without needing an introduction — twin ribbons of white dropping through the gorge in the middle distance, framed by cliffs of exposed rock and thick stands of pine. A weathered sign at the edge of the boardwalk pointed the way: HURRICANE FALLS TRAIL.

Sami waved at absolutely no one in particular, the way you do when a view is too good to keep to yourself, and set off down the trail to get a closer look at the falls the sign was so excited about.

## Hurricane Falls

The trail switchbacked down through the trees until it spilled out onto a wooden observation deck, and there it was: Hurricane Falls, dropping in two broad sheets over a jumble of dark, water-smoothed boulders before pooling into a churning green plunge pool. Above it all, almost impossibly high, the park's suspension bridge floated between the cliff walls, tiny figures crossing it like they were walking on a spider's thread.

Sami stood at the railing under the sign that read HURRICANE FALLS OBSERVATION and just listened for a while — the water didn't so much roar here as *insist*, over and over, on being the loudest thing in the gorge. He lifted the little camera from around his neck and took the kind of photo that never quite captures how loud a waterfall actually is up close, and was perfectly happy with it anyway.

## Tempesta Falls

<figure>
  <img src="/images/sami/tempesta-falls.jpg" alt="Sami the sourdough sandwich at South Rim Trail Overlook 7, looking out at Tempesta Falls, with a fellow sandwich traveler nearby" />
  <figcaption>Tempesta Falls, from Overlook 7 on the South Rim Trail.</figcaption>
</figure>

Back up on the South Rim Trail, a small wooden marker at Overlook 7 announced a view of Tempesta Falls, a long, elegant drop feeding into a deep turquoise pool below. Sami flipped open the guidebook to check a fact about the gorge's age — ten thousand years of the river doing this same patient work — and looked up to find he wasn't the only snack taking in the scenery. Another traveler, a fellow open-faced sandwich with a camera of his own, had stopped at the same overlook, and the two swapped a quick nod and a few words about trail conditions before each continued on at their own pace, the way good trail encounters tend to go.

Tempesta, Sami decided, was the kind of waterfall that made you want to just stand still for a minute. So he did.

## Oceana Falls

<figure>
  <img src="/images/sami/oceana-falls.jpg" alt="Sami the sourdough sandwich at Oceana Falls on the gorge floor, with the suspension bridge arcing overhead" />
  <figcaption>Oceana Falls, at the very floor of the gorge.</figcaption>
</figure>

The last stop required a permit and a fair number of stairs — a sign at the entrance to the Gorge Floor Trail made both facts clear — but Sami hadn't come all this way to stay up on the rim. Down at the bottom, Oceana Falls poured into a wide, glassy pool at the very floor of the canyon, with the suspension bridge arcing overhead and cliffs rising up on every side like the walls of a cathedral. This was as deep into the gorge as a sandwich could get, and it felt like it: cooler air, the smell of wet stone, the river close enough to touch.

Sami sat for a while at the railing, camera forgotten in his lap, and thought about how the whole gorge — the overlook, Hurricane, Tempesta, and now this — was really just one long river doing one patient thing over ten thousand years. He thought too about his own patient process, the long slow ferment that had gotten him here, and decided the two of them had more in common than most sandwiches would guess.

The climb back up out of the gorge was a long one, but Sami didn't mind. Some views are worth every one of the stairs.
