It all started, as so many questionable adventures do, with Bruce the Bald Baker and his beloved San Francisco Giants. Bruce is a long-suffering Giants fan — the kind of long-suffering that comes with decades of practice — and one evening, after watching his team swing at absolutely everything and hit absolutely nothing, he sighed and said the fateful words: *"What this team needs is bats."*

Now, Bruce had heard somewhere that the best place to find bats was a cave. And not just any cave — Mammoth Cave in Kentucky, the longest known cave system on planet Earth. So he packed a lunch (carefully, out of respect), bought two tickets, and dispatched his two most trusted explorers: Sami the Sourdough Sandwich and his sister, **Fari the Friendly Focaccia**.

A word about names, because this family takes them seriously. Sami's name is a wink at *semolina*, the golden durum flour of his ancestors. Fari's is short for *farina* — which is simply Italian for "flour." Their parents, the Grand Sourdoughs of San Francisco, believed a bread should always remember what it's made of.

## The Historic Entrance

The trail curved down through green Kentucky woods until a great stone archway opened in the hillside, exhaling a steady stream of cool air like the earth itself was sighing contentedly. Fari checked the sign — MAMMOTH CAVE NATIONAL PARK — and then checked her guidebook, which had opinions.

"It says here the cave has more than **426 miles of mapped passages**," she read, "and the explorers keep finding more. Nobody knows where it ends." She looked up at Sami. "We are looking for bats in four hundred and twenty-six miles of cave."

"Then we'd better get started," said Sami, who had inherited his optimism from a long line of breads that rose no matter what.

Down the stairs they went, out of the warm afternoon and into the cool dark, and the temperature dropped like a lid coming down on a Dutch oven.

## The Rotunda

The first great room took their breath away — a ceiling like a stone sky, wide as a ballpark, which Sami noted approvingly. The guidebook said that during the War of 1812, workers mined saltpeter right here in this room, and that the cave's name had nothing to do with woolly mammoths at all. *Mammoth* just meant **big**. The pioneers who found it needed a word for a cave with rooms like this, and "big" wasn't big enough.

"No mammoths," Fari confirmed, scanning the shadows. "So far, also no bats."

"They're probably farther in," said Sami. "If I were a bat, I'd want the good seats."

## Fat Man's Misery

The passage ahead narrowed, and narrowed, and then *really* narrowed, winding like ribbon candy between walls polished smooth by a river that had moved out ages ago. The sign called it FAT MAN'S MISERY, which both sandwiches chose not to take personally.

Fari — flat, flexible, built exactly like a bread designed to slide gracefully out of a pan — glided through sideways without so much as a scuff. Sami, a tall boule with a proud round crumb, got briefly and magnificently stuck, and had to be talked through it one wiggle at a time by a sister who was enjoying herself far too much.

"You know what this cave is?" Fari said, once Sami had popped free with a sound like a cork. "It's **fifty-four degrees** in here, all year round, at about **eighty-seven percent humidity**. Steady, cool, and damp." She spread her arms at the tunnel. "Sami. We are *inside a proofing box.*"

Sami stood a little straighter. It was true. He'd never felt so well-rested in his life.

## The Bat Gallery

And then, rounding a bend, they found them. Hanging from the ceiling in a quiet alcove, wrapped up in their own wings like tiny brown parcels: **bats**. Real ones. A small sign nearby read BAT HIBERNACULUM — DO NOT DISTURB, and a passing ranger stopped to whisper the details.

**Thirteen species** of bats live in the park, the ranger explained — including the endangered Indiana bat and gray bat — and they're more than 130 kinds of creature that call the cave's darkness home, from cave crickets to fish with no eyes at all. But the bats have been having a hard time: a disease called white-nose syndrome has been hurting them badly since it arrived, which is why visitors whisper near the roosts, keep their distance, and clean their shoes on the special mats on the way out — so nobody carries trouble from one cave to another.

Sami looked at the sleeping bats for a long moment. Then he looked at the baseball bat he'd been carrying the whole way, and quietly lowered it.

"These aren't the Giants' kind of bats," he whispered.

"No," Fari whispered back. "These ones are already on a team. And they're *not* to be recruited."

They tiptoed away and let the little brown parcels sleep.

They saved the showstopper for last: a cascade of golden stone pouring from the ceiling in ripples and curtains, a waterfall caught mid-plunge and frozen that way for the ages. The formations here grow drip by patient drip, the guidebook said, over thousands of years — which struck both sandwiches as a very familiar way of doing things.

"Slow fermentation," Sami said reverently.

"The slowest," Fari agreed. "This cave would have made a wonderful baker."

A century and a half ago, a guide named **Stephen Bishop** — who explored more of this darkness than anyone of his era, and crossed chasms no one had dared cross — called Mammoth Cave *"grand, gloomy, and peculiar."* Standing in the lantern-light with his sister, his crust cool and his spirits high, Sami decided that was also a fair description of his family, and he meant it as the highest compliment.

## The Report Back

Bruce the Bald Baker met them at the door with hopeful eyebrows. Sami delivered the news as gently as he could: the bats of Mammoth Cave were spoken for — hibernating, protected, and frankly not built for fastballs. The Giants would have to solve their swinging problem some other way, which, as Bruce noted, has been true for quite some time.

But the trip was hardly a loss. Fari pinned their first out-of-Georgia postcard to the kitchen map, Sami gave a full report on the underground proofing conditions (five stars, would ferment again), and both of them agreed that any cave with 426 miles of passages had certainly earned the name Mammoth — even if every last one of its bats intends to stay retired from baseball.
