![A finished braided loaf](/images/guides/braid_hero_finished.jpg)

A braid isn't merely decorative. Braiding changes the loaf's surface area, its crust-to-crumb ratio, how it expands during proofing and baking, and — not least — how it looks arriving at a table. It's also the shaping family that pairs most naturally with this site's **Enriched & Sweet** section: butter, eggs, and milk produce exactly the kind of strong, supple, roll-able dough that braiding wants, and the braid returns the favor with a tender, shreddable crumb that pulls apart along the strands. The [Brioche Base](/recipes/brioche-base) is, not coincidentally, a braid-ready dough.

One honest caveat before the patterns: with five or more strands, **there is no single universally accepted method** — several legitimate braiding systems exist for each strand count, and they produce visibly different (all correct) results. The methods below are chosen for being the most learnable, not for being the only truth.

---

## Part 1: The Right Dough

Braiding is a shaping exercise only if the dough cooperates. A slack 85% hydration country dough will fuse into an undifferentiated blob no matter how well you braid it. What braiding wants:

- **Moderate hydration** — roughly 60–65%, firm enough that strands hold their round cross-section.
- **Full gluten development** — strands are rolled, stretched, and woven; underdeveloped dough tears.
- **Extensible but not slack** — it should roll into a rope willingly and hold that rope's shape.
- **Enrichment helps** — butter and egg make the dough plastic and forgiving, and keep the strand definition sharp through the bake.

This is why enriched sourdough is the natural home for elaborate braids, and why the stiff-starter approach behind the [Brioche Base](/recipes/brioche-base) (see [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain)) fits so well here: a firmer, milder, yeast-leaning culture in a dough that needs its structure intact.

## Part 2: The Fundamental Rule — Positions, Not Names

Every braid starts the same way: strands laid out parallel, numbered left to right.

**1 2 3 4 5 6 7**

Here's the trick that makes every braid on this page learnable: **the numbers refer to positions, not to particular strands.** After every move, the strands renumber themselves by where they now sit. Don't think "take strand #3 and move it" — think "take the strand *currently in position 3*." A strand's identity doesn't matter; its position does. Internalize that one idea and even the seven-strand braid reduces to a short repeating chant.

And a beautiful pattern hides in the whole family:

| Braid | The outside strand moves | Concept |
|---|---|---|
| 3-strand | Over 1 | Outside → center |
| 4-strand | Over 1, under 1 | Over/under begins |
| 5-strand | Over 2, under 1 | — |
| 6-strand | Over 2, under 1 | — |
| 7-strand | Over 3, under 1 | — |

Every braid is the same move — an outside strand traveling toward the center through some over/under sequence, alternating sides — with only the counts changing.

### Preparing strands (all braids)

1. **Divide by weight.** Equal strands braid evenly and bake evenly; eyeballed strands don't. Use the scale.
2. **Pre-roll** each piece into a short log; rest 5–10 minutes. Dough that resists rolling hasn't relaxed yet — forcing it makes tapered-middle, fat-ended strands.
3. **Roll to final length**, center outward, all strands identical. Slightly longer than feels necessary — braiding eats length.
4. **Keep the bench only lightly floured.** Strands need a little grip to roll, and too much flour on their surfaces stops them adhering where the braid crosses — which is how gaps form.
5. Braid **snug but not tight**. The proof will plump every strand; a braid woven drum-tight tears itself apart in the oven.
6. **Pinch both ends firmly and tuck them under.** An untucked end unravels in the oven, and no amount of egg wash hides it.

### Difficulty at a glance

| Strands | Difficulty | Character |
|---|---:|---|
| **2** | ★ | Simple twist |
| **3** | ★ | The classic |
| **4** | ★★ | Woven, substantial |
| **5** | ★★★ | Intricate, elegant |
| **6** | ★★★★ | Dense weave |
| **7** | ★★★★★ | Elaborate |
| **8** | ★★★★★ | Basket-weave showpiece |
| **9** | ★★★★★ | Feathered grand braid |

## Part 3: Classic Braids (2–4 Strands)

### 2-strand twist

Really a twist rather than a braid, but it's the foundation and it's genuinely useful — rustic loaves, small rolls, twisted baguette-style breads.

Lay two strands side by side (or cross them in an X at the midpoint). **Right over left, then the new right over left, and repeat** to the ends. Pinch and tuck.

### 3-strand braid — master this one first

![Three strands joined and ready to braid](/images/guides/braid_3_strand_steps.jpg)

The classic, and the gateway to everything below. Positions **1 | 2 | 3**:

1. Move the strand in position **3 over position 2**.
2. Move the strand in position **1 over position 2**.
3. Repeat — outside right over the center, outside left over the center, alternating.
4. Pinch the ends, tuck them under.

The rhythm: **outside → center, outside → center.** Most people's hands already know it from braiding hair or rope; the only bread-specific skill is keeping the tension even.

![A three-strand braid coming together](/images/guides/braid_3_strand_in_progress.jpg)

The result is the familiar rope with three distinct ridges — right at home on challah-inspired breads, brioche-style loaves, and anything from the Enriched & Sweet section with cinnamon in it.

### 4-strand braid

![A four-strand braid in progress](/images/guides/braid_4_strand_steps.jpg)

The over/under weave begins. Positions **1 | 2 | 3 | 4**:

1. Outside right: **over one, under one** (position 4 travels over 3, under 2).
2. Outside left: **over one, under one** (position 1 travels over 2, under 3).
3. Renumber by position, repeat, alternating sides.
4. Pinch and tuck.

The chant: **outside → over one → under one → switch sides.** The result is broader and flatter than the 3-strand, with a genuinely woven look — a strong candidate for a centerpiece loaf without the difficulty of the five-plus braids.

## Part 4: Woven Braids (5–7 Strands)

This is where the position-numbering rule stops being a nicety and becomes the whole method. Work slowly, renumber after every move, and say the chant out loud — it genuinely helps.

### 5-strand braid

![Five strands joined and fanned, ready to weave](/images/guides/braid_5_strand_steps.jpg)

Positions **1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5**. The chant: **outside → over two → under one.**

1. Outside right strand: over two strands, under one — it lands near the center.
2. Outside left strand: over two, under one.
3. Alternate sides, renumbering each time, to the ends. Pinch and tuck.

![A finished five-strand loaf](/images/guides/braid_5_strand_finished.jpg)

The finished surface starts reading as woven textile rather than rope — intricate enough to impress, still manageable enough for a first attempt to succeed.

### 6-strand braid

![A six-strand braid in progress](/images/guides/braid_6_strand_steps.jpg)

Positions **1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6**. Same chant as the 5-strand: **outside → over two → under one → switch sides** — there's simply one more strand in the field, so the weave gets denser and the loaf gets taller.

Don't try to memorize the braid as a whole; nobody does. Trust the repeating move and the renumbering, and check your work every few passes — a mistake caught two moves back unweaves cleanly.

### 7-strand braid

![A seven-strand braided wreath](/images/guides/braid_7_strand_wreath.jpg)

Positions **1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7**. The chant stretches: **outside → over three → under one → switch sides.**

Seven strands is where braiding becomes genuinely impressive — and genuinely slow. Budget the time, keep the strands covered against drying if the kitchen is warm, and treat it as an advanced decorative project rather than a technique anyone needs before making good bread. Joined into a ring, it makes a spectacular wreath — a natural fit for the site's Holiday & Event tag.

## Part 5: Showpiece Braids (8–9 Strands)

At eight and nine strands the loaf stops reading as a braid at all and starts reading as fabric. These are presentation pieces — holiday tables, celebrations, the bake you bring when you're showing off (in the nicest way).

For braids this wide, written over/under instructions genuinely stop being the right teaching tool — the strand count makes prose ambiguous no matter how carefully it's written. The reliable approach is a **position-state diagram**: write the positions as a row of numbers, show the row after each move, and follow along one state at a time. (For the site, an animated or step-by-step illustrated sequence would serve readers better than any paragraph — worth building when these braids get their own recipe pages.)

### 8-strand braid

![A finished eight-strand woven loaf](/images/guides/braid_8_strand_finished.jpg)

Think of it as **two groups of four that continually interact**: outside strand travels inward through an over/under sequence, alternating sides, exactly as before — there's just more field to cross. The result is a remarkably broad basket-weave surface where individual strands almost disappear into the pattern.

### 9-strand braid

![A nine-strand braid before baking](/images/guides/braid_9_strand_unbaked.jpg)

Nine strands gives you something the even counts don't: a **center strand** (position 5 at the start) that acts as a visual anchor, with the braid developing symmetrically around it — work left, then right, weaving each outside strand toward that central axis. Depending on the exact system, the surface comes out feathered, woven, or herringbone-patterned.

![A finished nine-strand loaf](/images/guides/braid_9_strand_finished.jpg)

## Part 6: Proofing and Baking a Braid

Braids proof and bake by slightly different rules than the loaves in the [Proofing and Shaping guide](/guides/proofing-and-shaping):

- **Proof shorter than a boule.** You're balancing rise against definition: a fully-proofed braid plumps until the strands start merging and the pattern blurs. Aim for noticeably puffed but with valleys still clearly visible between strands — roughly 1.5x, not doubled.
- **Egg wash is the braid's best friend** — it's what turns the ridges deep mahogany against golden valleys and makes the pattern pop. Wash gently, into the crevices, without gluing strands together. For deepest color, wash once before proofing and again just before baking.
- **No scoring.** The braid's geometry does the expansion management a score would do; the strands are the controlled release.
- **Bake at enriched-dough temperatures** — braids are usually enriched, and their high surface area browns fast. 350–375°F territory rather than the 450°F+ of a lean Dutch-oven loaf; tent with foil if the ridges darken before the interior is done.

### Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix next time |
|---|---|---|
| Strands fused; pattern vanished | Dough too wet or too slack, overproofed, or braided too tight | Firmer dough (60–65%); proof to 1.5x only; braid snug, not tight |
| Braid unraveled in the oven | Ends not pinched and tucked | Pinch hard, tuck under, set the loaf on the tuck |
| Gaps between strands | Too much flour on strand surfaces, or braided too loose | Lightly floured bench only; keep the weave in contact |
| Strands tapered and lumpy | Rolled before the dough relaxed | Pre-roll, rest 5–10 min, then roll to length |
| Braid lost definition while proofing | Overproofed | Watch the valleys, not the clock |
| Pale valleys, burnt ridges | Oven too hot for an enriched braid | Drop to 350–375°F; tent with foil late in the bake |
