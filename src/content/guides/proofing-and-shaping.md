By the end of bulk fermentation, your dough has everything it needs to become great bread: a developed gluten network and thousands of tiny gas cells earned over hours of patient fermentation. What it doesn't have yet is *form*. Shaping and proofing are the two stages that turn a bin of lively dough into a loaf — and they're best understood as a pair, because one sets up the other. **Shaping determines what the dough is capable of becoming; proofing determines how successfully it gets there.** Together they decide volume, crumb structure, oven spring, how the score opens, and what the crust looks like when it's all over.

A one-line mental model worth keeping for the whole page:

> **Bulk fermentation builds the gas network. Shaping organizes it. Proofing expands it. Baking sets it.**

---

## Part 1: Why Shaping Matters

Shaping is not decoration. Three real things happen when you shape a loaf, and each one pays off later in the bake.

### It creates surface tension

The visible goal is a round or an oval, but the actual goal is **stretching the outer layer of gluten into a taut, continuous skin** — think of putting a tight skin around a soft, inflated balloon. That tension holds the dough together through final proofing, steers the rise *upward* instead of letting it slump outward, gives the loaf structural support during oven spring, and leaves a smooth surface that takes a clean score. Nearly every shaping technique on this page, from a boule to a nine-strand braid, is ultimately a different route to the same destination: tension without tearing.

### It organizes the crumb

Good shaping coaxes the gas cells built during bulk into an organized structure rather than letting them collapse or merge at random. This matters most with high-hydration dough, where the margin for error is thinnest — sloppy shaping there produces a loaf that's flat, dense, unevenly fermented, or gummy. But the opposite error is just as real: **aggressive shaping degasses the dough and throws away fermentation you spent all day building.** The touch you're after is confident but gentle — firm enough to build tension, light enough to preserve the interior.

### It sets the loaf's geometry

A boule, a batard, a baguette, and a sandwich loaf aren't just different silhouettes. Geometry changes how heat penetrates the dough, how much surface area becomes crust, how the loaf expands, how you score it, and the crust-to-crumb ratio of every slice. A baguette is mostly crust; a sandwich loaf is mostly crumb; a boule sits in between. Picking a shape is picking what kind of eating experience the flour ends up as.

## Part 2: Pre-Shape and Bench Rest — The Universal First Move

Every shape below starts the same way, so it's worth learning once rather than re-reading it seven times.

**Pre-shape.** Turn the fermented dough out and gently form it into a loose round (or a short cylinder, for long shapes), without pressing out the gas. You're not building final tension yet — you're giving a slack, irregular mass a preliminary organization to build on.

**Bench rest.** Let the pre-shaped dough relax, uncovered or lightly covered, for **15–30 minutes**. The gluten you just tightened needs time to loosen its grip before it will stretch again willingly; skip the rest and the final shape fights you, tears, or springs back. The exact time depends on dough strength and temperature — a strong, cool dough needs the full half hour, a slack warm one may be ready sooner.

**Final shape.** Now the real work: fold, tighten, and build that taut outer skin. The shape-by-shape instructions live in Part 4.

The rhythm to internalize: **fold → tighten → create surface tension.** If a shaping attempt goes wrong, you can usually pre-shape again, rest again, and retry — dough is more forgiving than it looks, though each round of handling costs a little gas.

## Part 3: Proofing — The Final Rise

**Proofing is the final fermentation period after shaping and before baking.** The culture keeps working, the shaped dough expands, and the gluten network stretches around the growing gas cells. The entire challenge is stopping at the right moment — and the single biggest beginner mistake is treating that moment as a fixed number of minutes.

> **Proof according to the condition of the dough, not the clock.**

Temperature, starter strength, flour, hydration, salt, and everything that happened during bulk all shift proofing time — which is exactly the territory of the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration). Warm dough proofs in a fraction of the time cold dough does, and a whole-grain dough runs faster than a white one at the same temperature.

![Shaped dough on its final rise in a banneton](/images/guides/dough_proofing_in_banneton.jpg)

### Reading the proof

**Underproofed** dough hasn't accumulated enough gas. The tell in the oven: explosive but uncontrolled spring, large tearing around the score, dense patches in the crumb, big tunnels near the top over a tight bottom — the "volcano loaf."

**Properly proofed** dough has enough gas and enough extensibility to expand beautifully: good spring, an open but coherent crumb, controlled bloom at the score, good volume.

**Overproofed** dough has fermented past what its gluten can contain — and at the molecular level, this is the acid-driven gluten breakdown described in [Part 1 of the Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration). The dough feels weak and fragile, spreads instead of springing, the score barely opens, and the crust can bake up pale and thin.

### The poke test

The classic hands-on check for final proof: flour a fingertip and press gently about half an inch into the shaped dough.

- Springs back **quickly and completely** → underproofed, give it more time.
- Springs back **slowly and only partway**, leaving a slight dimple → ready. Bake.
- **Doesn't spring back at all**, or the dough sighs and deflates → overproofed. Bake immediately anyway — a slightly overproofed loaf baked *now* beats one that proofs even further while you agonize.

It's a judgment call that gets sharper with reps, which is exactly what the [Baking Log](/resources/baking-log) template is for — record the poke result and the crumb outcome, and your finger calibrates itself within a few bakes.

### The cold retard

Most recipes on this site proof shaped loaves in the refrigerator (38–40°F) for 8–16 hours, and it's worth knowing why that works so well. Cold nearly stops yeast activity while acid development creeps along — so you buy flavor complexity without proportional gas production. Cold dough also scores dramatically better: firm, taut, and unsticky, it takes a clean confident cut where room-temperature dough drags. The toll is time under acid, which is why a long retard needs gluten strength banked up front — the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) covers that trade in detail.

One practical note that confuses new bakers: shaped loaves proof **seam-side up** in a banneton (so the smooth side lands facing up when you turn the loaf out to score it), but **seam-side down** on a couche or a pan, where there's no flip before baking.

## Part 4: The Shape Library

The fundamental shapes, and where each one earns its keep:

| Shape | Also known as | Best suited to |
|---|---|---|
| **Boule** | Round loaf, cob | Round artisan loaves — the Dutch oven classic |
| **Batard** | Bâtard, oval loaf | Oval artisan loaves — most of this site's Classics |
| **Baguette** | (Ficelle when very thin) | Long, thin, crust-forward breads |
| **Sandwich loaf** | Pain de mie, Pullman | Clean-slicing pan bread |
| **Rolls & buns** | — | Individual portions |
| **Couronne** | Crown | Ring loaves — holiday and celebration bakes |
| **Fougasse** | — | Flat, decorative, all-crust bread |
| **Braids** | Tresse | Enriched doughs — see the [Braiding guide](/guides/braiding) |

Shaping is a reusable skill, not a per-recipe instruction — master a shape once and every recipe that calls for it is already half-learned.

### Boule — the fundamental round

![Building surface tension on a boule](/images/guides/boule_shaping_steps.jpg)

The boule is the best shape to learn first: it's the most forgiving, it teaches the tension-building motion every other shape reuses, and it's what [John Dough](/recipes/john-dough) — the site's reference dough for The Classics — most naturally wants to be.

1. Pre-shape into a loose round; bench rest 15–30 minutes (Part 2).
2. Flip the dough so the smooth side is down and the seam side faces up.
3. Fold the bottom edge up to the center, then the sides in, then the top down — a rough envelope.
4. Turn it over and cup your hands around the far side of the ball, dragging it toward you across an unfloured patch of bench. The slight friction tightens the surface.
5. Rotate a quarter turn and drag again. Repeat until the skin is smooth and taut — stop before it tears.
6. Place **seam-side up** in a floured round banneton.

![A shaped boule proofing in a spiral banneton](/images/guides/boule_proofed_spiral_banneton.jpg)

The drag-and-rotate in steps 4–5 is the single most transferable motion in shaping. Once your hands know it, the batard is a variation, not a new skill.

### Batard — the workhorse

![Rolling and sealing a batard](/images/guides/batard_shaping_steps.jpg)

The elongated oval is arguably the most *useful* artisan shape — excellent volume, and easier to score well than a boule (one confident cut down the long axis, and the shape rewards it with an ear).

1. Pre-shape into a loose round; bench rest.
2. Gently flatten the center into a rough rectangle, preserving the gas at the edges.
3. Fold the bottom third up.
4. Fold the left and right sides in toward the center.
5. Starting from the top, roll the dough down toward you, pressing lightly with each roll to seal as you go.
6. Seal the final seam with the heel of your hand.
7. Rock and roll the cylinder gently to even it out and elongate slightly, tapering the ends if you like that look.
8. Place seam-side up in an oval banneton.

![A batard on its final proof in an oval banneton](/images/guides/batard_in_oval_banneton.jpg)

The goal is **tension along the entire length** — a batard that's tight in the middle and slack at the ends will bake up lopsided.

### Baguette — progressive elongation

![Elongating a baguette under both palms](/images/guides/baguette_shaping_steps.jpg)

Baguettes ask for a genuinely different strategy: a long, narrow cylinder built *without* crushing the interior. The secret is patience — **progressive elongation**, never one heroic stretch to final length.

1. Divide the dough; pre-shape each piece into a short cylinder; bench rest.
2. Flatten very gently into a rectangle.
3. Fold lengthwise — top third down, press to seal; bottom third up over it, seal again.
4. Fold in half lengthwise one more time and seal the seam firmly with the heel of your hand.
5. Roll the cylinder under both palms, starting from the center and working outward.
6. Lengthen gradually over two or three passes, letting the dough relax a moment between passes rather than forcing it.
7. Taper the ends.
8. Proof seam-side down in the folds of a floured couche.
9. Score with several overlapping cuts angled nearly parallel to the loaf's long axis.

![Baguettes proofing in a floured couche](/images/guides/baguettes_on_couche.jpg)

### Sandwich loaf — uniformity over drama

![Rolling a sandwich loaf into an even cylinder](/images/guides/sandwich_loaf_shaping_steps.jpg)

Pan bread flips the priorities: the pan supplies the structural support, so **uniformity matters more than extreme surface tension**. What you want is an even crumb that slices cleanly — which means this is the one shape where deliberately degassing the big bubbles is correct, not a mistake.

1. Pre-shape into a loose round or rectangle; bench rest.
2. Gently press out the largest bubbles.
3. Flatten into a rectangle roughly as wide as your pan is long.
4. Fold the sides in toward the center.
5. Roll tightly from one end to the other, keeping the roll even.
6. Seal the seam and place **seam-side down** in a greased loaf pan.
7. Proof until the crown rises about an inch over the pan rim (for an open-top loaf), then bake.

![A sandwich loaf proofing in its pan](/images/guides/sandwich_loaf_proofing_in_pan.jpg)

This is the shape the approved Soft Sandwich Loaf Base is built around — both its plain and enriched variants shape identically; the dough inside is what changes.

### Rolls and buns — the same physics, miniaturized

![Dividing and rounding rolls](/images/guides/rolls_division_and_rounding.jpg)

Everything above applies at a smaller scale, with one new technique: **rounding**. After dividing (use the scale — equal weights proof and bake evenly, and eyeballed rolls never do), pre-round each piece, rest briefly, then:

1. Place a piece seam-side down on an unfloured patch of bench.
2. Cup your hand over it, fingertips and heel touching the surface like a cage.
3. Move your hand in small, quick circles. Friction between dough and bench spins the surface tight.
4. When the ball is smooth and taut underneath your palm, it's done — tray it, proof, bake.

![Rounding a roll under a cupped hand](/images/guides/roll_rounding_technique.jpg)

If the dough just slides around instead of tightening, the bench is too floury — the technique *needs* a little grip to work.

### Couronne — the crown

![A couronne proofing in a ring banneton](/images/guides/couronne_in_ring_banneton.jpg)

A ring loaf that earns its place on holiday tables — dramatic to look at, and with more crust per slice than a boule of the same weight. The simplest method:

1. Shape a boule, then punch a hole through the center with floured fingers.
2. Gently widen the hole, rotating and stretching evenly, until the ring is open enough that the hole won't close during proofing and oven spring (wider than looks right — it shrinks from both directions).
3. Proof in a floured ring banneton, or on parchment with an oiled, oven-safe bowl or ramekin sitting in the hole to hold the shape.
4. Score around the ring; bake.

An alternate construction arranges several small balls of dough in a ring around a central opening — they proof and bake into a pull-apart crown.

![A finished couronne](/images/guides/couronne_finished.jpg)

### Fougasse — the flat one

![Cutting the slits in a fougasse](/images/guides/fougasse_cutting_slits.jpg)

Fougasse breaks the pattern of everything above: it's **flat rather than volumetric**, and the cuts that make it look like a leaf are doing structural work, not just decoration — they turn the interior into edge, making fougasse essentially all crust. If someone in the house always fights for the heel of the loaf, this is their bread.

1. Gently stretch the dough into an elongated oval or leaf shape on parchment.
2. Cut one long slash down the center (not through the edges), and several diagonal cuts branching off it, like leaf veins — a bench scraper or pizza wheel works better than a knife.
3. Open the cuts wide with your fingers — much wider than looks right, since they close up considerably as the dough proofs and springs.
4. Top if desired (olive oil and flaky salt at minimum; olives, herbs, or cheese in the dough itself).
5. Proof briefly — 20–30 minutes, not hours; there's no volume to build.
6. Bake hot and fast.

![A finished fougasse](/images/guides/fougasse_finished.jpg)

Fougasse is also the most forgiving possible home for dough that's slightly overproofed for a lofty shape — flatness is the point, so the usual penalty doesn't apply.

## Part 5: Shaping and Scoring — Two Halves of One System

A connection that's easy to miss: **shaping establishes the tension; scoring tells that tension where to release.** The score is a deliberately engineered weak point in the taut skin you built. In the oven, the chain runs heat → steam → gas expansion → gluten stretch → the score opens along your cut instead of the loaf bursting at random.

This is why identical scoring patterns behave completely differently on differently-shaped loaves. The dramatic "ear" on a batard isn't produced by the blade — it's produced by surface tension, with the blade merely deciding where. A slack, poorly-shaped loaf scored by a master still bakes up blunt; a well-shaped loaf scored plainly still blooms.

## Part 6: Troubleshooting

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix next time |
|---|---|---|
| Loaf spreads flat | Insufficient shaping tension, weak dough, or overproofed | Rebuild tension in final shape; check proof with the poke test; see the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) if it's a fermentation issue |
| Explosive spring, torn score, tunnels up top | Underproofed | Proof longer; trust the poke test over the clock |
| Little spring, score won't open | Overproofed, or weak surface tension | Shorten the proof; shape with more conviction |
| Huge tunnel just under the crust | Aggressive shaping trapped a seam of large bubbles, or uneven fermentation | Gentler, more even pressure during final shape |
| Torn surface during proofing | Overtightened skin, or shaping a dough that needed more bench rest | Ease up on the final drag; give the gluten its rest |
| Dough tears during shaping | Underdeveloped gluten, or dough too cold and tight | More development in bulk; let refrigerated dough warm slightly before shaping |
| Dense, tight crumb | Excessive degassing during shaping, or underfermented bulk | Handle lighter; verify bulk by percent rise, not time |
| Dough won't hold any shape | Hydration too high for the flour's strength, or overfermented | Drop hydration 5%; see the flour/hydration interplay in the [Four Levers guide](/guides/ph-flour-hydration) |
| Rolls bake up uneven | Eyeballed division | Weigh every piece |
