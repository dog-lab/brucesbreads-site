Every choice you make in sourdough — hydration, timing, temperature, shape — is really a negotiation with the flour you picked before any of it started. Flour decides how much water the dough can carry, how fast it ferments, how open the crumb can get, and what the finished loaf actually tastes like. The four-levers guide covers *why* flour behaves the way it does (protein, bran, enzymes, buffering); this one is the practical field guide: what each flour on the shelf is good for, how to blend them, and how the clock moves when whole grains join the party.

---

## Part 1: How to Read a Bag of Flour

Two numbers matter more than anything else printed on the bag, and only one of them is usually printed on the bag.

**Protein percentage** is the printed one — it's your rough proxy for gluten-forming potential, and therefore for how much water the flour can absorb and how much structure it can build. But treat it as a starting estimate, not a verdict, for two reasons:

**Brands vary more than categories do.** "All-purpose" is not one thing. King Arthur guarantees its AP at 11.7% protein — which sits at the *floor* of many bread-flour definitions — while Southern soft-wheat brands (the biscuit heroes) run closer to 8–9%. A recipe that behaves beautifully with one brand's AP can run slack and sticky with another's. When a table below says "all-purpose: 10–12%," read it as "check your actual bag."

**Protein quantity is not protein quality.** Whole wheat routinely out-proteins bread flour on paper and still builds a weaker dough, because bran physically interferes with the network (more below). Einkorn posts respectable protein numbers attached to gluten so fragile it barely counts. The number tells you how much raw material is present; the flour's genetics and milling decide what that material can actually do.

The second number — **extraction rate**, how much of the whole grain made it into the bag — usually isn't printed at all in the US, but it's the one that governs fermentation speed, water absorption, and flavor depth. White flour is low-extraction (bran and germ sifted out); whole wheat is 100% extraction; and the interesting middle ground ("high-extraction" or "Type 85" flour) comes up in the blending section below.

## Part 2: The White Wheats — Your Structure

### Bread flour (12–14% protein)

The workhorse, and the foundation of nearly every recipe on this site. High gluten-forming potential builds a strong, elastic network that traps gas well, tolerates high hydration (75–85%+), and — crucially for sourdough — **withstands long acidic fermentation**. Acid activates the protease enzymes that slowly snip gluten apart (see the four-levers guide, Part 1), and a strong network simply has more structure in reserve to lose before the loaf notices. That's why a long cold retard wants bread flour underneath it.

One genuine caveat from the open-crumb literature: more protein is not always better. Push past ~13–14% into high-gluten territory for a standard loaf and the crumb turns chewy and tight — the network gets so strong it resists the very expansion you're trying to encourage. For an open, tender artisan crumb, an 11–13% white flour is the sweet spot; the site's recipes live there.

**Flavor:** mild, clean, faintly sweet and creamy — a neutral stage that lets the starter's lactic and acetic character do the talking.

### All-purpose flour (10–12% protein, brand-dependent)

A softer gluten structure and lower absorption (typically happiest at 65–72% hydration). Perfectly capable of good sourdough — especially a strong AP like King Arthur's 11.7%, which behaves like entry-level bread flour — but it has less structural reserve for very wet doughs or very long ferments. It also ferments slightly faster than bread flour, since its starch is a bit more accessible to enzymes.

**Flavor:** neutral and soft — the least assertive flour on this page.

## Part 3: The Whole Wheats — Flavor with a Clock Attached

### Whole wheat / hard red wheat (13–15% protein)

The trap flour, and worth understanding properly because the trap is avoidable. On paper it out-muscles bread flour; in the bowl, the bran's sharp particles physically cut gluten strands as the dough is worked, and the bran itself takes up space in the matrix. The result is a network weaker than the protein number promises: denser crumb, lower spring, unless you compensate.

Three compensations, all worth doing:

- **More water.** Bran is thirsty — whole-grain doughs want roughly 10–20% more water than their white-flour equivalents, and absorb it slowly.
- **More rest.** A long autolyse (60–120 minutes for whole-grain-heavy doughs) lets the bran hydrate and soften *before* it starts sawing at your gluten. Softened bran cuts less.
- **A white-flour foundation.** See the blending section — this is what blending is for.

**Fermentation:** fast. Whole wheat carries the grain's full complement of minerals, enzymes, and wild microbes, all of which push the culture harder. This is the whole reason the site's recipe spec drops DDT to 75°F for whole-grain-heavy doughs.

**Flavor:** nutty, robust, earthy, slightly tannic from the bran — the flavor argument for tolerating everything above.

### White whole wheat / hard white wheat (12–14% protein)

Same 100% extraction, same bran behavior and fast fermentation — but milled from hard *white* wheat, whose bran carries fewer of the bitter tannic pigments. The result is a milder, sweeter, lighter-colored whole grain that makes an excellent bridge flour: whole-grain nutrition with training wheels for the flavor.

## Part 4: Rye and the Heritage Wheats — Character Flours

### Rye (whole/dark, medium, or light; ~9–12% protein, but that's not the point)

Rye's protein is mostly *not* the glutenin/gliadin pair that builds elastic gluten — it can't form a real network. What rye has instead is **pentosans**: gum-like carbohydrates that absorb enormous amounts of water and hold gas the way a thick batter does, not the way a gluten balloon does. That's why high-rye doughs are sticky rather than stretchy, and why 100% rye is a genuinely different craft from wheat baking rather than a harder version of it.

Rye is also the most enzymatically active flour here — amylase levels high enough that an insufficiently acidic rye dough digests its own starch into gum in the oven. The fix is deliberate sourness: rye wants dough pH around 4.0–4.5, which is exactly why traditional rye breads are *sour* breads. The four-levers guide (Part 2) covers rye's narrow pH window in detail.

**In small doses, though, rye is a gift.** At 5–10% of the flour, it behaves as a flavor multiplier — minerals and enzymes that energize fermentation, moisture retention that improves keeping quality, and a deep, malty, faintly fruity tang — all without enough network disruption to matter. This is why the site's standard starter feeds on a white/rye blend.

**Flavor:** deeply earthy, tangy, malted, slightly spicy; the more acidic the dough, the more its signature character shows.

### Spelt (12–15% protein)

An ancient hexaploid cousin of modern wheat with plenty of protein but gluten that's all extensibility and little elasticity — spelt doughs stretch beautifully and spring back barely at all. They relax quickly, spread easily, absorb water fast, and over-proof without much warning. Kept to 20% or less of a blend, spelt donates sweetness and a tender crumb; pushed past that, drop hydration, shorten the ferment, and consider a loaf pan as scaffolding.

**Flavor:** mildly sweet and nutty, with a subtle almost-cinnamon warmth.

### Einkorn (the oldest wheat there is)

Humanity's first domesticated wheat — diploid, never hybridized, and carrying gluten so fragile it's best treated as a flavoring rather than a structure. Einkorn absorbs water slowly and dislikes much of it (60–65% hydration territory as a majority flour), ferments quickly, and breaks down fast when over-proofed. Blended at 10–20% into a bread-flour dough, none of that matters and you get its remarkable flavor for free.

**Flavor:** rich, buttery, toasty — arguably the best-tasting wheat in this guide, which is why bakers keep putting up with it.

### Durum / semolina (12–15% protein)

The pasta wheat, and the highest-protein wheat species commonly available — but its gluten runs tight and short rather than stretchy and elastic, so it builds a firm, fine-textured crumb rather than big open holes. Finely re-milled durum (*rimacinata*) is the form meant for bread, and it's the engine of the great Italian golden loaves (pane di Altamura among them): vibrant yellow crumb from natural carotenoids, crackly crust, and a moderate-to-slow fermentation that's refreshingly calm after the whole-grain flours above.

**Flavor:** sweet, buttery, nutty — close to fresh pasta or toasted corn.

## Part 5: The Shelf at a Glance

| Flour | Protein | Comfortable hydration | Network | Ferment speed | Flavor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bread flour** | 12–14% | High (75–85%+) | Very strong, elastic | Steady | Mild, sweet, creamy |
| **All-purpose** | 10–12%* | Medium (65–72%) | Moderate | Slightly faster | Neutral, soft |
| **Whole wheat (hard red)** | 13–15%* | High (needs 10–20% extra water) | Medium — bran-disrupted | Fast | Nutty, earthy, tannic |
| **White whole wheat** | 12–14% | High (same as above) | Medium — bran-disrupted | Fast | Milder, sweeter whole grain |
| **Rye** | 9–12%* | High (sticky, gum-bound) | Minimal — pentosans, not gluten | Very fast | Tangy, malted, earthy |
| **Spelt** | 12–15% | Moderate (goes slack) | Extensible, weak elasticity | Fast | Sweet, nutty, warm |
| **Einkorn** | Reads high* | Low (60–65% as majority flour) | Very weak | Fast | Buttery, rich, toasty |
| **Durum (rimacinata)** | 12–15% | Medium (70–75%) | Strong but tight/short | Moderate–slow | Sweet, pasta-like, golden |

*\*Asterisked numbers are the ones the bag will mislead you on: AP varies widely by brand; whole wheat, rye, and einkorn all post protein figures their functional gluten can't cash.*

## Part 6: Blending — The Foundation-and-Accent Model

Here's the baker's paradox: the flours with the most flavor build the least structure, and the flour that builds the best structure tastes the most polite. The resolution isn't choosing — it's blending. Think of it as **foundation + accent**: a strong white flour supplies the gluten balloon, and whole/heritage grains supply the character.

| Goal | Foundation (bread flour) | Accent (whole/heritage) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open crumb, easy handling** | 90% | 10% | The beginner-friendly ratio — nearly white-flour behavior, noticeably better flavor |
| **The professional standard** | 80% | 20% | Most of this site's blends live here — real flavor depth, still very manageable |
| **Rustic and complex** | 70% | 30% | Advanced territory: faster clock, denser crumb, needs the Part 7 adjustments in full |

### Why the ratios work

The white foundation is the balloon: an elastic network that traps CO₂. The accent grains are, structurally speaking, scissors — bran cutting strands, weak gluten diluting the network. Below ~30% accent, the balloon absorbs the damage; much past it, the scissors start winning and the crumb tightens noticeably. (A 40%+ whole-grain loaf can absolutely be great bread — it's just a *different* bread, denser and moister by design, not an open-crumb loaf that fell short.)

A few accent-specific rules of thumb:

- **Rye punches far above its weight.** 5–10% delivers obvious flavor and fermentation energy; it never needs to be 30% of a wheat-style loaf.
- **Spelt and einkorn are sweetness donors** — lovely to 20%, volume-killers beyond it.
- **Hard red whole wheat is the structural accent** — of all the whole grains, it contributes the most real gluten alongside its bran, so it's the one to reach for when pushing toward 30%.

### The high-extraction shortcut

If you want whole-grain flavor without whole-grain consequences, **high-extraction flour** (often sold as "Type 85" or "bolted" flour) is the honest cheat: flour with *some* of the bran sifted out. You keep most of the mineral and flavor payload while shedding the worst of the gluten-cutting bran, and it behaves close to a strong white flour in the dough. Where a recipe calls for an 80/20 blend, T85 at 100% lands somewhere similar in spirit — deeper flavor, nearly white-flour handling.

## Part 7: The Whole-Grain Clock — Adjusting Bulk Fermentation

Add 20% whole grain to a familiar white recipe and the recipe's timeline is now fiction. More minerals, more enzymes, more wild microbes: the culture runs hotter, and gluten degradation runs faster right along with it. Here's the adjustment kit.

**Expect bulk to shorten by roughly 15–30%.** A 100% white bread-flour dough that takes 4.5–6 hours at 75°F will, at 20% whole grain, generally wrap up in about 3.5–4.5 hours at the same temperature. At 30% whole grain, shave further still.

**Lower your rise target, not just your time.** A white dough can safely ride bulk to a full doubling; a 20% whole-grain dough headed for a long cold retard should be shaped at more like a **30–50% rise**, because acid load and enzymatic breakdown keep accruing in the fridge and this dough has less structure in reserve to spend. (This slots directly into the temperature-and-percent-rise method in the four-levers guide, Part 4 — same system, adjusted target.)

**Trust the dough's signals over any clock:**

- Rounded, convex edges where dough meets bowl, with visible bubbles at the surface and perimeter
- A cohesive, sponge-like jiggle when the bowl is shaken — not sloshy, not inert
- Aerated and slightly tacky but still holding tension; excessively sticky, weak, or soupy means it's already past the exit

**Keep the dough cool.** DDT of 74–77°F, per the site's standard 75°F target for whole-grain-heavy doughs. At 80°F+, warm dough and whole-grain enzymes compound each other, and starch and gluten degrade before gas production peaks.

**Front-load your strength-building.** Bran damage accumulates over time, so bank the structure early: stretch-and-folds or coil folds every 20–30 minutes for the first two hours, then leave the dough alone for the final stretch so gas cells can expand undisturbed.

**Or slow the whole clock down.** If you'd rather keep your familiar 5–6 hour rhythm, reduce the starter: where a white dough takes 20% inoculation, a 20% whole-grain dough at 12–15% starter lands back on roughly the original schedule. Less fuel, same fire.

## Part 8: Troubleshooting by Flour

| Symptom | Likely culprit | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Whole-grain dough feels tight, dry, or tears | Bran hasn't finished drinking | Add the extra 10–20% water; autolyse 60–120 min |
| Whole-grain loaf dense despite good fermentation | Accent ratio too high for the goal | Shift toward 80/20; use hard red as the accent; add a white-flour foundation |
| Blend dough turned sticky and slack late in bulk | Whole-grain clock ran out early | Shorten bulk 15–30%; lower rise target; cool the dough to 75°F |
| Spelt or einkorn dough spreads flat | Weak-gluten flour over its structural budget | Cap at 20% of the blend; drop hydration; pan-bake high-percentage versions |
| Rye-heavy crumb gummy, top collapsed | Amylase ran wild — dough not acidic enough | Use a properly sour rye levain; target pH 4.0–4.5 (four-levers guide, Part 2) |
| AP-flour dough can't hold shape at recipe hydration | Brand's protein below the recipe's assumption | Check the bag; drop hydration ~5%; or step up to bread flour |
| Crumb chewy and tight in a high-protein bake | Too much network for an open crumb | Blend down toward 11–13% protein; raise hydration slightly |
