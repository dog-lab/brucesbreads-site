*Part 1 of the Bruce's Breads Starter Guide. See also: [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter), [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours), and [Starter vs. Levain](/guides/starter/starter-vs-levain).*

![Day 1 initial mix — flour and water just combined in a jar](/images/guides/01_day1_initial_mix.jpg)

Every loaf on this site starts the same way: flour, water, and time. A sourdough starter is nothing more exotic than a jar of flour and water left to ferment until it hosts a stable, self-sustaining culture of wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria — but "nothing more exotic than" undersells it a little, because that jar is what leavens and flavors everything else here. Get it established once, and it can outlive the jar it started in.

This guide walks through building one from scratch. If you already have a starter going and just need to keep it healthy, skip ahead to [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter).

## Tools You'll Need

- A wide-mouth glass jar with a loose-fitting lid — a one-pint (500ml) canning jar works well. Glass lets you actually see the fermentation happening, which matters more than it sounds like it would.
- A digital kitchen scale. Volume measurements (cups) are too imprecise for this — weigh everything in grams.
- A small, sturdy spatula for stirring and scraping down the jar.
- A rubber band or piece of tape to mark the starter's height after each feeding, so you can see how much it's risen.
- Somewhere warm to keep it. A turned-off oven with the light on (checked with a thermometer so it doesn't get too warm), a proofer, or just a warm spot on the counter all work.

## Water

Use unchlorinated water if you can. Chlorine and chloramine (both used to disinfect municipal tap water) can slow fermentation. If your city uses chlorine, letting a jug of tap water sit out uncovered overnight lets most of it dissipate. If your city uses chloramine instead, that trick doesn't work — use filtered or bottled water instead. Distilled water isn't recommended, and reverse-osmosis water gets mixed reviews from home bakers since it can strip minerals the culture wants; if that's what you have, it's worth trying, but switch sources if your starter seems sluggish for no other reason.

## Flour: Why Whole Grain Matters at the Start

This is the one part of starter creation worth getting right before you begin, and all three of the sources this guide draws from agree on it: **don't start a new culture on 100% white flour.**

Wild yeast and the lactic acid bacteria that ferment alongside it live on the outer hull of the wheat berry — the bran and germ that get milled away when flour is refined into white flour. Whole-grain flour still has that hull in it, so it's carrying the "bugs" your starter needs. White or bread flour, on the other hand, is more easily broken down into the simple sugars those microbes eat — it's the "food." A blend of the two gives the culture both what it needs to get going and what it needs to keep eating once it does.

Whole wheat and rye both work; rye in particular tends to kick-start activity a little faster thanks to its higher nutrient content, which is why it shows up as the preferred choice across all three source guides this page draws from. Spelt and einkorn also work in a pinch. What doesn't reliably work is starting from 100% white or all-purpose flour alone — it's not that it's impossible, but the failure rate climbs enough that it's not worth the risk on your first attempt.

For the creation process below, mix a batch of **50% whole wheat flour and 50% bread flour (or all-purpose flour)** — you'll use this blend for every feeding during the build. A pure rye starter or a higher rye percentage also works well if you'd rather lean into that; adjust to taste once you understand the basic mechanics. (Worth knowing now: this generous whole-grain share is a *creation* blend, not a forever recipe — once the culture is established, maintenance feedings shift to a mostly-white mix with a smaller whole-grain kicker. The why behind that switch is covered in [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours).)

## The Method

There's more than one well-documented way to build a starter, and the honest answer is they all work — they just trade off differently between speed, hand-holding, and margin for error. This guide's default method leans on **The Sourdough Journey**'s watch-and-wait approach, because it's the most explicit about *why* each day looks the way it does and gives you a way to diagnose a stalled starter rather than just hoping it comes around. **The Perfect Loaf**'s temperature and tooling guidance is folded in throughout. If you'd rather have a shorter, lower-fuss version, **The Bread Code**'s method (linked at the end of this page) gets you there in about a week with almost no troubleshooting built in — it just asks you to trust the process a bit more on faith.

### Days 1–3: Activating the Microbes

**Day 1 — The Initial Mix.** Combine 75g of your 50/50 flour blend with 75g of water in your jar. Stir vigorously — you want a thick, homogeneous paste, thick enough that it wouldn't pour out if you turned the jar upside down. Scrape down the sides, cover loosely, and set it somewhere slightly cool if possible (around 68°F/20°C for this first mix). You're rehydrating and waking up the dormant yeast and bacteria that were already sitting on the whole wheat flour's hull.

**Day 2 — A Few Bubbles.** Check in 24 hours later. You should see the first small bubbles forming on the surface — a good sign. Don't be alarmed by strange smells at this stage (cheese, alcohol, even something closer to dirty socks); that's normal early-stage fermentation, not a failure. If you see a layer of clear liquid separating out, that's just water separation in a young, weak starter — not "hooch" (true hooch, the boozy liquid mature starters produce, can't really happen this early). Stir it back in and give it more time.

**Day 3 — The False Rise.** ![Day 3 false rise — vigorous frothy bubbling on the surface](/images/guides/02_day3_false_rise.jpg) Around 48 hours in, you'll often see a burst of vigorous, frothy bubbling — sometimes enough to make the mixture rise noticeably in the jar. This is deceiving: it's not your yeast population taking off, it's a bloom of less-desirable bacteria burning through the flour before the culture stabilizes. Your starter can smell genuinely unpleasant here. **Don't discard or feed yet** — resist the urge, even though it looks ready. Give it the full 72 hours from the initial mix before your first discard-and-feed. This patience is what actually gets a starter off to a strong start; jumping in early to "help" is the single most common way people accidentally weaken a new culture.

### Days 4–6: The Quiet Days

After the false rise, don't be surprised when things go quiet. This is completely normal — the undesirable bacteria have burned off, and the yeast population is now doing the slow work of actually establishing itself.

**Day 4 — First Discard and Feeding.** At 72 hours, discard down to 25g of starter and feed it 50g of your flour blend plus 50g water. Stir well, scrape the jar, mark the starter's height with a rubber band. This is a **1:2:2 ratio** (one part starter, two parts flour, two parts water by weight) — you'll repeat this same ratio through the rest of the build. From here on, try to keep the starter a bit warmer, ideally 75–80°F (24–27°C); it'll still work cooler, just more slowly, but avoid holding it above 80°F/27°C for long stretches, since that tends to push it acidic faster than it's ready for. Don't save any discard from these early days — it can carry undesirable bacteria you don't want in a pancake batter later.

**Days 5–6 — Watching for Bubble Activity.** Each day, check for surface bubbles before you feed — not on a fixed clock, but based on what you actually see. If there's no bubble activity, stir and wait another 12–24 hours rather than discarding on schedule anyway; discarding a starter that hasn't shown renewed activity removes most of the young yeast population you're trying to build, and it has to rebuild that population by roughly 400% just to get back to where it was. Once you do see clearly increased bubbling over the previous day, discard to 25g and feed 50g flour / 50g water again, same as Day 4.

### Days 7–10+: Slow Strengthening

**Day 7 — The First Real Rise.** ![A mature, ripe starter doubled in volume with a domed top](/images/guides/03_ripe_mature_starter.jpg) By now you should start seeing your starter actually rise in the jar after a feeding, not just bubble on the surface. Wait until it clearly peaks and begins falling — or, if it plateaus without falling, until the peak has clearly held for a few hours — before you feed again. When it's past peak, discard to 25g and feed 50g/50g as before.

**Days 8–10 and beyond.** Keep repeating the discard-and-feed cycle, watching for the starter to peak faster each day than it did the day before — that accelerating rise time is the clearest sign of a strengthening culture. Note the room temperature too; a warm starter will naturally rise faster than a cool one, and that's not a strength difference, just a temperature one.

### Is It Ready?

The standard readiness test: your starter should roughly **double in volume within 4–6 hours** of a fresh 1:1:1 feeding (equal parts starter, flour, and water) at around 74°F/23°C. Using the 1:2:2 ratio from this build process, expect more like 4–7 hours to double. A cooler starter isn't weaker for taking longer — it's just cooler; the same strong starter that doubles in 6 hours at 74°F might take 10 hours at 65°F/18°C.

Look for the rise speed more than the height — a starter that reliably doubles in 4 hours is stronger than one that triples in 8. You want to see it pass this doubling test for **three consecutive days** before trusting it for a real bake. Once it does, it's ready to use, though expect timing to still run a little slower than a fully mature starter for its first 30 days or so as it keeps strengthening. Once your starter passes the test, it's safe to start saving discard for pancakes, waffles, and the like.

Once established, keep a small backup jar of your starter in the back of the fridge as insurance — replace it monthly. If something ever goes wrong with your main jar, you've got a fallback rather than starting completely over.

### A Note on Mold

Mold risk is real but manageable during the first 10 days, and drops sharply once the starter's acidity stabilizes. You're looking for the unmistakable kind — classic gray, fuzzy mold, the same thing you'd see on stale bread. Don't confuse it with whole wheat flour's natural dark specks, pinkish hue, or the white bubbles that form on the surface as it ferments — those are all normal. If you do spot actual mold, the whole batch needs to go; sterilize your tools and start over. It's uncommon, but it happens.

## Troubleshooting the Build

**Nothing's happening at all by Day 7.** Your starter may have hit a "stall point" — check The Sourdough Journey's dedicated troubleshooting page (linked below) for the stall point test before assuming failure.

**No bubble activity at all since Day 1.** This is the one case where restarting makes sense — try again with a fresh bag of whole wheat flour.

**It smells awful.** Expected in the first several days (cheese, alcohol, even worse). This settles down as the culture stabilizes. If it still smells off after 10+ days of otherwise normal activity, see [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter) for troubleshooting a mature starter instead.
