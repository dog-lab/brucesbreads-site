*Part 4 of the Bruce's Breads Starter Guide. See also: [Creating a Starter](/guides/starter/creating-a-starter), [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter), and [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours).*

These two words get used almost interchangeably in casual conversation, and the fermentation happening inside both is the same. But they serve different jobs in a bake, and knowing the difference is what unlocks a lot of flexibility — including the stiff-starter approach behind the [Brioche Base](/recipes/brioche-base) and the [Laminated Pastry Base](/recipes/laminated-pastry-base) (both built this way, both approved). See [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours) for that hydration background before this page; this one builds on it.

## The Short Version

| Sourdough Starter | Levain |
|---|---|
| An ongoing culture, refreshed on a regular schedule | A one-time offshoot, made for a single bake |
| Never used up entirely — some is always kept back to keep the culture alive | Used up completely — all of it goes into the dough and gets baked |
| Consistent flour makeup and hydration, since it's fed the same way every time | Can be any flour, any hydration, any ratio — built fresh for what that recipe needs |
| Has a stable, predictable flavor over time | Can be tuned to shift the flavor or performance of one specific loaf |

Both are **preferments** — flour, water, and a leavening culture mixed ahead of time and left to ferment before going into a final dough. A starter is the ongoing "mother" culture. A levain is a small, purpose-built batch spun off from that starter for one bake, with the freedom to look nothing like the starter it came from.

## Why Bother Building a Levain?

If a recipe's preferment needs match your starter's normal flour, hydration, and quantity, you can skip the extra step and mix straight from the starter — plenty of bakers do exactly that, and it's not wrong. A levain earns its place for two specific reasons:

**1. Scaling up.** If a recipe calls for more preferment than you happen to have ripe starter on hand for, a levain lets you build up to that amount without disturbing your maintained culture's own feeding rhythm.

**2. Changing the flavor or performance profile — for one bake only.** This is the more interesting reason, and the one that matters for this site's enriched and laminated bases. Rather than permanently altering how the standing starter is fed, a levain lets you dial in exactly what a specific dough needs, then return to normal maintenance afterward.

A levain made with a higher percentage of whole-grain flour tends to push the finished bread more sour. A levain made with **100% white flour** does the opposite — white flour lowers the mixture's buffering capacity, so pH drops faster during fermentation, which limits how much souring bacterial activity has time to build before the levain's ready to use. The practical result is a milder, gentler final flavor — exactly what a delicately flavored, sweet, or enriched bake wants, where you don't want the tang of a fully whole-grain-fed starter competing with butter, sugar, and egg. (A white-flour levain built for a babka is the textbook example of this technique.)

Flour choice, hydration, fermentation time, temperature, and how much ripe starter you seed it with are all separate levers — you can pull any combination of them to land where a specific bake needs.

## Building a Levain for This Site's Enriched and Laminated Bases

This is the direct payoff of the last three pages, and it's now landed for two of the three: **the [Brioche Base](/recipes/brioche-base) and the [Laminated Pastry Base](/recipes/laminated-pastry-base) both use exactly this approach** — a stiff (45–50% hydration) starter, torn into pieces and softened directly in cold liquid (milk and eggs for Brioche, milk alone for Laminated Pastry) before the flour goes in (see each recipe for its full mixing technique). Brioche runs 40% of flour weight; Laminated Pastry runs 20%, a lighter preferment load since that détrempe isn't meant to carry a strong sour flavor. Bruce's own instinct — confirmed against real technique, not just a hunch — was that these enriched and laminated doughs are better served by something built more deliberately than the standard starter:

- **Stiffer hydration** (50–65%, see [Starter Types and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours)) for a firmer, more yeast-dominant, less acidic culture that doesn't fight a dough already carrying a lot of butter and egg, or a laminated dough's delicate folded structure.
- **A higher percentage of white flour** in the levain build itself, leaning toward the milder-flavor end of the spectrum described above — appropriate for enriched, less aggressively sour breads.

## A Quick Levain in a Pinch

Forgot to build a levain ahead of time? Using the ripe standing starter directly is always a fallback — it won't hit the exact flavor profile a purpose-built levain would, but it's better than not baking. Alternatively, a fast intermediate levain — **equal parts flour, water, and ripe starter (1:1:1)** — ripens in about 3–4 hours at 78–80°F/25–26°C, giving a middle-ground option when there's no time for a slower, more deliberate build.

## How Much Levain Goes in a Dough?

The levain's share of a recipe's total flour (its "prefermented flour percentage") commonly runs **6–30%** across a wide range of bread styles, though individual recipes can fall outside that range depending on the formula. This is a different number than the *levain percentage* sometimes quoted in a baker's-percentage table — see the note on baker's percentages in the site's System Prompt reference material if the distinction matters for a specific recipe.

## Multiple Levain Builds (Advanced Aside)

Some traditional methods use more than one levain build in sequence — building, ripening, then using part of that first levain to seed a second build, and so on — to further shift the yeast-to-bacteria balance or to fit a tighter production schedule. The Detmolder three-stage method for rye bread and the layered "refreshment" builds used in traditional panettone are both examples. Not something this site needs today, but worth knowing the term if it comes up in future research.
