I've been talking about stiff starters on this site for a while — the firmer, 45–50%-hydration cousin of the loose, spoonable culture that's fed every loaf here since day one. Talking about them is easy. On August 6, 2026, I finally put one to work for real, across three of my enriched bases at once. Two of the three were still on hold as I first wrote this, for two completely different reasons, and one of them very nearly ended with a pile of laminated dough discard and a bruised ego. This is the story of that week, in three acts — one of which has since gotten its ending.

## Act One: Brioche, which didn't put up a fight

Brioche went first, and it was almost anticlimactic. The reasoning is straightforward enough: a liquid starter left to run past peak tends to lean acetic, and acid works against exactly what a butter-and-egg-heavy dough needs most — a gluten network strong enough to trap steam under 50% butter and a five-egg load. A stiff starter ferments milder and more lactic, behaves more like a firm pre-ferment than a loose culture — closer to an Italian pasta madre or a French biga — and that firmness plays nicely with a dough that's supposed to build real structure before the butter ever goes in.

I switched the Brioche Base to a stiff starter at 40% of flour weight, reviewed it, and approved it that same day. It's the first recipe on this site to use a stiff starter for real, rather than just as something I'd been meaning to try. And here's the part that should have been the whole story: it worked. No drama, no crisis, no 2am diagnostic session. Just a good loaf.

Which is exactly why I wasn't calling it finished yet. It worked — but I didn't actually know whether it worked *better* than my standard starter would have. Curiosity is a legitimate reason to hold a release, so that's what I did: held Brioche back until I could run an actual side-by-side, stiff starter against standard, and see whether the difference was real or whether I'd talked myself into one.

**Update, August 21, 2026 — I ran it.** Same enriched dough, same bake day, only the starter swapped: 40%-of-flour-weight stiff starter against my standard loose culture, both baked as the sandwich loaves I actually eat this bread as. The verdict wasn't subtle. The stiff-starter loaf's crumb was noticeably better — tighter and more even — and its rise came in roughly double the height of the standard-starter loaf. Both loaves tasted good; I wouldn't have turned either one down. But taste being a wash while crumb and rise aren't isn't a coin flip anymore. Stiff starter wins, decisively, on the two attributes that actually matter most for a sandwich loaf. Brioche — Well Bread, since that's what this base shipped as — isn't running on a hunch anymore. It's confirmed.

## Act Two: Laminated Pastry, which fought back

This is the one that got away from me.

The Laminated Pastry Base got the same stiff-starter treatment as Brioche that same day — my first time actually trying the technique on a dough that also has to survive a butter block and two days of cold folding, not just sit in a mixer. The logic held up on paper: a stiff starter's slower, milder ferment suits a dough that's about to spend a day or two in the fridge between folds anyway, and its low water content matches a détrempe that's supposed to stay cool and firm. I brought it in, reviewed the base, and moved on.

Then I actually baked it, and it fell apart. One pain au chocolat, left to proof for over twelve hours, showed virtually no rise. The dough — for that and for a batch of cinnamon rolls made from the lamination discard — was bone dry, hard enough to work with that it cracked repeatedly during lamination instead of folding cleanly, tearing straight through to the butter block and leaving me with a lot more discard than pastry.

I went looking for the obvious suspects first, and ruled every one of them out. The starter was properly ripe — dome flattened, pH 4.1, squarely in the healthy range. The proofing spot was genuinely warm, 78°F sliding down to 72°F over those twelve-plus hours, right in the window the base itself calls ideal. I'd even used the recipe's optional insurance yeast, which should leaven reliably almost regardless of what the wild culture is doing — and it barely rose either. When your backup plan also fails, the leavening isn't your problem anymore.

That left the dough itself, and the math backed it up: the détrempe was sitting at roughly 37% effective hydration once you account for the stiff starter's own flour and water. Most commercial-yeast croissant and Danish doughs run 55–60%. I'd been so focused on the case for a stiff starter that I hadn't questioned whether the water number underneath it was actually reasonable — and it wasn't. It was dry enough to resist fermentation almost as much as it resisted rolling.

So I fixed it in stages, because I wanted to actually test each step rather than guess my way to a number. First bump: milk up to 205g, 41% baker's percentage, about 42% effective. Tested it two days later — better, tasted good even — but the crumb was still dry and still cracking on the later folds. Not there yet. Second bump: 225g, 45%, about 45.5% effective. That one worked. Flaky crust, no longer dry, proofed properly on schedule. The dough that had spent twelve hours doing nothing came back a few points of hydration later and behaved like it was supposed to the whole time.

The leftover dry dough from that first failed batch didn't go to waste, for what it's worth — worked back on the mixer with a splash of milk added in, it became a small rescue brioche braid instead. Even a failed bake doesn't have to be a wasted one.

The stiff starter, in the end, was never actually the problem. The water was. It just took a cracked butter block and a very patient twelve-hour proof to prove it.

## Act Three: Sandwich Loaf, the quiet asterisk

The enriched Sandwich Loaf variant got the calmest treatment of the three. I reviewed it that same day against a version I'd hand-drafted myself, and decided the site's existing draft was better as written — so it stayed on the standard starter, no switch needed. Confirmed, not even a live question, right next to two other bases where the stiff-starter case had just been made.

But "confirmed" and "released" aren't the same thing here, for a reason that has nothing to do with stiff starters at all. Every sandwich loaf I've baked to date used an earlier version of my standard starter — before I settled on the current 70% bread flour / 30% rye maintenance blend. I don't expect the rye to change anything; sandwich bread is about as forgiving a format as this site has. But "I don't expect a problem" and "I've verified there isn't one" are different sentences, and until I've baked this base with the starter I actually run now, I'm not calling it finished on a hunch.

## Where it stands

Three bases, one starter switch, three different outcomes: one that just worked and whose curiosity is now settled — stiff starter, confirmed — one that broke badly enough to teach me something real about hydration math, and one that's fine in every way except that I haven't technically checked. If there's a theme, it's that "the stiff starter works" and "the recipe is done" turned out to be two separate claims every single time — and the second one is always the more honest question to keep asking. Two of the three have now answered it.

The technical side of all this — what a stiff starter actually is, how to build one off your existing culture, and the multi-feed and rye-blend math that came out of this same week — lives in [Starter Types, Hydrations, and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours/).
