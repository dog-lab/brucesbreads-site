I made a promise in the last post. Writing about the San Francisco sourdough dynasty — Boudin, Parisian, Toscana, Colombo, and a Basque outfit called Larraburu Brothers — I had to admit something a little embarrassing for a native son: I don't remember Larraburu at all. Neither does my mother, and she was there for its entire run. I said then that Larraburu deserved a post of its own someday. Today's that day, and the deeper I dug, the more that opening confession started to look less like a personal gap and more like the whole story.

Because here's the thing about Larraburu Brothers: by most measures available, it wasn't a minor player. A 1964 New York Times reporter called it "the largest producer of sour dough bread in this area — and in the world, according to the management," turning out more than 75,000 pounds of bread a day. Departing travelers at San Francisco International were buying roughly 1,500 pounds of it a week just to take home. It shipped to Hawaii, Alaska, a distributor in New York, and — per one enthusiastic online account — even to France, which is a genuinely funny thing for an American bakery to pull off. And twelve years later, when two Kansas State food scientists wanted an actual, real-world sample of San Francisco sourdough to run through a gas chromatograph, Larraburu is the bakery they called. This wasn't a beloved neighborhood shop nobody outside the block had heard of. This was, quite possibly, the biggest sourdough operation on the planet. And somehow it still managed to disappear almost completely from the memory of a family that lived through its entire run three miles away.

## Two Basque brothers and eighty years of a starter older than expected

The Larraburu brothers arrived in San Francisco from Basque country in 1896, the same wave of Gold Rush-era professional bakers that gave the city Parisian (1856) and Toscana (1895) — men who took the accidental sourness the miners had been living with and turned it into deliberate craft. They brought their own starter with them, the way Boudin's people did, and set up shop in the Richmond District, on 3rd Avenue between Geary and Clement.

The business passed to Harold "Hal" Paul Sr. and a couple of partners in 1945, with Paul becoming sole owner in 1958. Under his stewardship, Larraburu didn't stay a neighborhood bakery — it became a wholesale machine. Paul's nephew reportedly had the instinct that made it happen: get the bread onto Fisherman's Wharf, into restaurants like Alioto's, into the airport gift shops where a returning tourist could grab a loaf for the trip home. It worked spectacularly. By the time the New York Times came calling in 1964, Larraburu was baking around the clock, and the general manager — Hal Paul Jr. — was telling reporters that visiting Frenchmen preferred San Francisco sourdough to the loaves back home. Whether or not that's exactly true, it's a hell of a thing for a Basque immigrant family's bakery to have people saying about it in print.

And it had fans in odd places. Alfred Hitchcock is rumored to have sent his assistant up from Los Angeles on a private plane specifically to bring back Larraburu loaves for his Hollywood parties. Herb Caen, the columnist who more or less invented the idea of "San Francisco culture" as a beat, reportedly called Larraburu's French sour bread part of "the quintessential San Francisco meal." This was a bread people organized parties around.

So why didn't it stick in my family's memory the way Boudin did? I think the wholesale strategy is the answer, and it's a little poignant once you see it. Boudin built a destination — a bakery you visit, a story (the bucket, the earthquake) you get told while you're standing in line for it. Larraburu built distribution — a truck route, a wire rack at the Wharf, a shrink-wrapped loaf in the SFO gift shop. Both approaches can make you the biggest bread in the world. Only one of them makes a six-year-old remember your name. My mother's own map of San Francisco sourdough had Boudin at the center and Colombo on it. Parisian and Larraburu, despite being every bit as real and every bit as good, simply weren't brands she was loyal to — even though, in one of those only-in-San-Francisco coincidences, she once lived directly across the street from the Cliff House, where Parisian was served on every table. You can live in the middle of a city's bread culture and still not know the name of the biggest bakery in it.

## What actually happened inside that bakery, twenty-four hours a day

This is the part of the Larraburu story I didn't expect to enjoy as much as I did — because unlike most vanished bakeries, we don't just have legend and nostalgia to go on here. We have an actual peer-reviewed chemistry paper, built from real samples taken directly from the Larraburu ovens, plus newspaper accounts from the bakery's own management, plus decades of home bakers on forums like The Fresh Loaf trying to reverse-engineer the whole operation from those same scraps. And — refreshingly, given how tidy the legend usually gets — the sources don't fully agree with each other. I find that more honest than a story that resolves too neatly.

The bakery ran continuously, three shifts a day, every day, because that's what 75,000 pounds of bread a week requires. At the center of it was the Mother — a single continuous starter sponge, rebuilt roughly every eight hours around the clock, that by some accounts had been alive and fed without interruption since 1896. By the time I would have been old enough to notice Larraburu bread at all, that starter was pushing eighty years old — meaning it predated California's admission to the union by nowhere near as much as I initially assumed, but was still, comfortably, older than my mother.

Here's where it gets genuinely interesting to a home baker: **the sponge stage had no salt in it at all.** The Mother was rebuilt purely from previous sponge, flour, and water, fermented warm — around 80°F — for nine to ten hours before ever touching the final dough, where the salt (1.5–2%) finally got added. If that sounds familiar, it's because it's the exact scenario Guide 05 on this site, [Salt: The Quiet Fifth Lever](/guides/salt-timing-and-gluten/), spends several thousand words explaining the consequences of: without salt around to suppress them, wheat proteases — enzymes that break down gluten protein into amino acids — get to work largely unchecked, and they're most active in exactly the low-pH, warm conditions a long sourdough ferment produces. Larraburu wasn't doing this on purpose as a flavor technique, as far as anyone's found — it's simply what a 24-hour commercial bakery's rebuild schedule looks like at that scale. But it turned their Mother into an accidental, decades-long experiment in exactly the kind of unchecked proteolysis that guide describes in the abstract.

That's not a guess on my part — it's the actual finding of the paper. In 1978, two Kansas State food scientists (working with a researcher from Cairo's Al-Azhar University) published "Lactic and Volatile (C₂–C₅) Organic Acids of San Francisco Sourdough French Bread" in *Cereal Chemistry*, using sponge, dough, and finished-bread samples obtained directly from the Larraburu Baking Company. Every previous study of San Francisco sourdough had found exactly one volatile organic acid worth mentioning besides lactic acid: acetic. This team, using a more sensitive extraction method, found six more — propionic, isobutyric, butyric, α-methyl n-butyric, isovaleric, and valeric acid — the first time anyone had detected them in this bread. Together they make up less than 1% of the total acidity, but the researchers traced their likely origin straight to proteolysis: specific amino acids released by gluten breakdown (leucine, isoleucine, valine, threonine, methionine) get further broken down through transamination and decarboxylation into these specific minor acids. A bakery that ran its unsalted Mother warm for nine or ten hours, every eight hours, for decades, had — without anyone setting out to prove anything — created the most proteolytically "cooked" starter anyone had ever put through a gas chromatograph. There's a small, strange poetry in the idea that Larraburu's most lasting scientific contribution wasn't a recipe. It was accidentally running the control group for an experiment nobody at the bakery knew they were conducting.

The pH numbers tell the same story a different way: the sponge dropped from 4.58 fresh to 3.80 after eight hours, the final dough from 5.03 down to 3.85 after its own four-hour proof, and the finished bread landed at 3.9. Baking barely moved the pH at all — but it did strip out about 9% of the dough's titratable acidity, almost entirely because acetic acid is volatile enough to partially cook off in the oven. Which means, if you want a slightly deflating fact for your next dinner-party bread tangent: the crumb of a Larraburu loaf was measurably less sour than the dough that went into making it. Some of that famous tang simply evaporated on its way to the table.

The rest of the process reads like a snapshot from a very different scale of baking than anything on this site. Final dough: roughly 60% hydration, mixed from 12%-protein flour, 1.5–2% salt, using only 15 parts of that sponge (about 9% pre-fermented flour) against 100 parts fresh flour. Divided and shaped freeform onto canvas dusted with corn meal or rice flour. Then — and this is the part that would make most home sourdough advice sheets flinch — proofed for four full hours at 105°F and 96% relative humidity, before baking at 420°F for 40 to 50 minutes in Perkins ovens whose shelves were lined with carborundum (silicon carbide), with low-pressure steam injected directly into the chamber. That hot, humid proof and that steam-and-stone oven combination is almost certainly where the "dark, thick, hard" crust came from that people who actually ate this bread describe — one relative of the family recalled edges hard enough that "our parents would use it to teethe us." It's a completely different proofing environment than anything a home Dutch-oven setup can replicate, which is worth knowing before anyone (myself included, eventually) gets too confident about "authentically" recreating this loaf in a Georgia kitchen.

And here's the part I promised wouldn't resolve too neatly: even the primary sources can't agree on the Mother's actual feeding ratio. The 1978 science paper says the sponge was built from 100 parts flour, 50 parts water, and 50 parts previous sponge — a 1:1:2 starter-to-water-to-flour ratio. But at least two U.S. patent documents describing the San Francisco sourdough process — the kind of document a bakery itself might file — describe a sponge rebuilt from two parts previous sponge, two parts flour, and one part water: a 2:1:2 ratio, with the Mother making up twice as much of the mix. A blogger who claims to have kept a piece of Larraburu's own lineage alive on Maui quotes the formula from memory as "two parts previous sponge, two parts high-gluten flour, one part water — hold seven to eight hours, rebuild," which matches the patent version, not the peer-reviewed one. Home bakers piecing this together on The Fresh Loaf forum have spent actual years cross-referencing the 1964 newspaper account against the 1978 science paper trying to reconcile the numbers, and haven't fully managed it either. I like that this doesn't tie up. A bakery this large, this documented, and this scientifically studied still can't tell you with certainty exactly how its own most famous ingredient was fed. That's not a failure of research. That's just what happens to institutional knowledge that lived in a baker's hands and a rebuild schedule instead of a written recipe.

## The accident, the lawsuit, and the overnight ending

Larraburu's ending wasn't a slow fade — it was abrupt, and it followed real tragedy. In the early 1970s, a Larraburu delivery truck struck and severely injured a six-year-old boy. The company, according to family accounts, had only recently emerged from an earlier bankruptcy, and the resulting lawsuit revealed it was badly under-insured for a judgment of that size. Paired with the debt already on the books, the litigation was more than the business could survive. Larraburu Brothers closed for good in May 1976, after eighty years, essentially overnight.

There's something worth sitting with in that timeline before moving on: a company that had spent decades building itself into what may have been the largest sourdough bakery on Earth was gone within a few years of one truck accident. Scale doesn't protect you from that kind of ending. Neither, as it turns out, does it guarantee you'll be remembered — which brings us back to where this whole post started.

One curious footnote on timing, worth flagging honestly rather than glossing over: that 1978 *Cereal Chemistry* paper was presented at a meeting in October 1977 and lists its samples as coming "from Larraburu Baking Company (San Francisco, CA 94107)" — more than a year after the bakery's May 1976 closure. I couldn't pin down exactly how that reconciles; it's possible the samples were collected before the closure and simply took time to work through publication, or that some smaller-scale operation continued briefly during the bankruptcy proceedings. Either way, there's a decent chance that paper is the last analytical record of the actual bread from a company that had already stopped existing by the time anyone read it in print — which, if true, is a strangely fitting scientific eulogy for a bakery that otherwise left so little written down.

## The starter people are still chasing

![A cartoon sourdough starter sprinting across a wheat field toward a farmhouse, kicking up a trail of flour dust](/images/blog/larraburu/the-starter-that-got-away.jpg)

*No photograph of the actual fugitive exists. This is close enough.*

Here's where the story stops being history and starts being an active mystery, one that people are working on right now.

A live sourdough culture fed continuously for eighty years doesn't just cease to exist because the storefront closes — somebody scoops a jar of it before the ovens go cold. That's exactly what appears to have happened, more than once, along more than one thread.

The best-documented lineage: sometime in the 1970s, before the closure, Hal Paul reportedly gave a portion of the Larraburu starter to David Bernhard, a third-generation owner of Portland's Helen Bernhard Bakery, who wanted to try sourdough baking up north. That starter was still there years later when a baker named Scott Hessler came through Helen Bernhard's kitchens, secured his own sample, and kept it "under lock and key" for years, by his own account. When Hessler's friend and baking protégé Ben Baker left Hawaii for a chef position in Austin, Texas, Hessler gifted him a piece of it — with one condition: never give it away. Baker has been baking with it in Austin ever since, most recently as executive chef at Miraval Austin's Hilltop Crossings Kitchen, and shipped loaves back to San Francisco for the city's 2015 Panama-Pacific International Exposition centennial celebration.

The person chasing all of this down is Julia Lavaroni, grand-niece of Hal Paul Sr., who has spent over a decade researching whether any authentic sample of the Larraburu mother survives, and is co-producing a documentary about the search called *Finding Larraburu*. In 2014, she flew to Austin with a friend to actually taste Baker's bread against her own childhood memory of the real thing. Her verdict was honest rather than triumphant: the crumb was right — "creamy and sour, but not like 'turn-up-your-nose' sour... a perfect tanginess" — but the crust wasn't quite there, which makes total sense once you know Larraburu's crust came out of a carborundum-lined, steam-injected commercial oven that no home or restaurant kitchen is going to fully replicate. Baker, for his part, never claimed to be recreating Larraburu exactly — by his own account, he was "about loving what the 'mother' did for his bread," not chasing an exact facsimile. Hessler has since acquired the rights to the Larraburu name itself and, according to Lavaroni's reporting, is planning to bring the bread back to market as "Larraburu Sourdough, The People's Bread."

That's one lineage, reasonably well documented. There's at least one more, less pinned-down thread out there: a separate personal account describes a piece of the starter having made its way to Hawaii and been kept alive on Maui, independent of the Bernhard/Hessler/Baker chain, with a taster describing it as carrying "wheat and time and something faintly cool and creamy underneath" — a description I find myself unreasonably moved by for a sentence about fermented flour. Whether that's the same lineage under a different telling, a genuinely separate surviving sample, or an unverifiable claim, I can't say with confidence, and I'd rather be honest about that than force the pieces to fit into one tidy narrative.

And then there are the home bakers who don't have access to any of these lineages at all, working purely from the public record — the Fresh Loaf forum threads where people have spent since at least 2019 cross-referencing the Galal paper against 1960s newspaper clippings and old patent filings, baking test loaves at Larraburu's proofing temperature just to see what happens, arguing amiably in the comments about whether a five-hour hot ferment overproofs a modern flour. That's citizen science in the most literal sense, and it's more or less exactly what I've spent this post doing myself — chasing down primary sources for a bread I never got to taste, using the tools I have instead of a jar I don't.

## Where that leaves this Georgia kitchen

I can't claim any lineage to the real Larraburu starter, and after everything above, I'm genuinely not sure who could claim it with full certainty either. What I can take from this bakery, three thousand miles and fifty years removed, is the *conditions* — the long, warm, unsalted sponge ferment that this site's own Guide 05 already explains the mechanism for, sitting right there in a piece of commercial baking history I hadn't connected it to until I went looking. My starter in its jar in Adairsville isn't descended from the Larraburu Mother. But every time I let a levain run a little longer, a little warmer, before the salt finally goes in, I'm running a small-scale version of the same experiment that bakery ran by accident for eighty years, twenty-four hours a day, without ever publishing a word about it themselves.

The bread is gone. The starter is scattered across at least two continents and several competing claims, none of them fully verifiable. But somewhere in a filing cabinet at Kansas State, there's a gas chromatograph readout that captured exactly what that dough was doing, chemically, in the last years before it disappeared — which, for a bakery that otherwise left almost nothing in writing, might be the most durable thing Larraburu Brothers ever produced.
