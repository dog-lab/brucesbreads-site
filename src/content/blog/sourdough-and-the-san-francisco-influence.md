*The founding post of the Bruce's Breads blog.*

I should tell you up front: I'm not a neutral party here. I was born and raised in San Francisco, and for people like me, sourdough isn't a food trend or a pandemic hobby. It's the smell of the city. It's what bread simply *was* before I knew bread could be anything else.

Some of my earliest memories are trips to Fisherman's Wharf with my mom — crab or shrimp cocktails and hunks of what I called "French bread," because I was too young to pay attention to branding and, as you'll see, the city itself spent a century calling it that too. I've lived in Georgia long enough to love it here, but those memories still creep back every time I bite into a slice of my Georgia-raised sourdough. Part of my palate was set in San Francisco fog before I could ride a bike, and this post is my attempt to explain why — and to sort the true parts of the legend from the parts the city made up about itself, which it absolutely did.

## It didn't start in San Francisco. It started next to the beer.

Sourdough is the oldest leavened bread there is. The Egyptians were baking naturally fermented bread thousands of years ago, almost certainly by happy accident — brewing and baking lived side by side, wild yeast got where wild yeast gets, and somebody noticed their flatbread had opinions and let it keep rising. Every leavened loaf on Earth descends from that lucky contamination. For most of human history, "sourdough" wasn't a style of bread. It was the *only* way bread rose.

So no, San Francisco didn't invent sourdough. What San Francisco did — and this is the honest version of the claim — was make it *famous*, keep it alive when the rest of America abandoned it, and stamp its name on the microbiology itself. Hold that last thought; it's the plot twist.

## The Gold Rush: romance, filth, and bread you could mistake for a rock

  <img src="/images/blog/sf-sourdough/prospector-carrying-loaf.jpg" alt="A Gold Rush prospector on a rocky mountain trail, pick over his shoulder and a sourdough loaf tucked under one arm" />
  <figcaption>Provisions, in order of priority: pick over one shoulder, loaf under the other arm.</figcaption>

In 1849 the world poured into San Francisco chasing gold, and every one of those miners needed to eat. The prospectors carried crocks and pouches of starter into the camps and up into the Sierra — fed daily, guarded jealously, and on cold nights taken *into the bedroll*, because a frozen starter was a dead starter and a dead starter meant no bread. Men were nicknamed "sourdoughs" after the thing they slept with. As a native, I find this entirely reasonable behavior.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/sf-sourdough/prospector-sleeping.jpg" alt="A prospector asleep in his tent at night, cradling his sourdough starter barrel like a hot water bottle" />
  <figcaption>Not a pillow — a payroll. A frozen starter meant no bread, so the starter got the warm spot.</figcaption>
</figure>

Now, here's where the record gets deliciously messy. Depending on what you read, Gold Rush sourdough was either wholesome frontier fare — the BBC's telling leans "nutritious and delicious" — or, in Atlas Obscura's memorable framing, *really gross*: sour by accident rather than intent, poorly risen, baked over campfires by men who had never baked in their lives, occasionally streaked yellow with desperate doses of baking soda. One forty-niner remembered miners' bread as "sorry, sad stuff." Another account jokes about loaves indistinguishable from the surrounding geology.

Both versions are true, and the contradiction is the point. The miners *kept the tradition alive*, but they mostly made bad bread — they were miners, not bakers, and 19th-century Americans generally considered sourness a baking *failure*. The bread that made San Francisco famous came from somewhere else entirely: the professionals who followed the miners.

## Enter the French: Isidore Boudin and the mother dough

Among the thousands who arrived in Gold Rush San Francisco was Isidore Boudin, of a family of French master bakers. Where the miners had accidental sourness, Boudin had *levain* — centuries of French technique for raising bread on a maintained wild culture, deliberately, with control. The story the city tells is that he married French method to the local wild culture the miners were using, and the result was something new: a loaf with real structure, a shattering crust, and that unmistakable tang — sold as "French bread" and eventually, once the city learned to love the sourness on purpose, as *sour French bread*. (See? I wasn't wrong at the Wharf. Just early.)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/blog/sf-sourdough/prospector-bakery.jpg" alt="A busy Gold Rush camp bakery beside a river, miners shaping dough near a crock labeled Mother 1853" />
  <figcaption>The professionals arrive: a camp bakery whose mother dough already outranks most of the claims.</figcaption>
</figure>

Boudin's bakery opened in 1849 and it is still operating today, which means you can walk into Fisherman's Wharf right now and buy a loaf raised on a mother dough the company claims has been continuously fed since the Gold Rush. When the 1906 earthquake and fire came for the city, the story goes that Louise Boudin fled with the mother dough in a bucket while the bakery burned. San Franciscans do not consider this an overreaction. She saved the family business, because the business *was* the bucket.

My mother — past ninety now, still eating sourdough, and the reason I was at Fisherman's Wharf with a shrimp cocktail in the first place — has never wavered on this subject. Boudin sourdough and crab legs at the Wharf was the family ritual, and if you ask her today she'll tell you flatly: *"It's the best."* More than nine decades of bread-eating stands behind that verdict, and I'm not going to argue with her. You shouldn't either.

Boudin wasn't alone, though. Parisian (founded 1856), Toscana (1895), Colombo (1896, across the bay in Oakland), and a Basque outfit called Larraburu Brothers (1896) built the city's great sourdough dynasty — mostly French, Italian, and Basque professionals selling European craft with a San Francisco accent.

## The bakery that vanished — and the starter people are still hunting

Larraburu deserves a post of its own someday. From a bakery on 3rd Avenue between Geary and Clement in the Richmond District, Larraburu Brothers became one of the most beloved sourdough producers in the world — the smell of it baking was one of the signature "smells of San Francisco," and Alfred Hitchcock supposedly had their bread on his shopping list when he was in town. Then in May 1976 it all ended, suddenly and tragically: a Larraburu delivery truck struck a young boy, the judgment revealed the company was badly under-insured, and the bakery went bankrupt almost overnight.

Here's a confession that says something about how fame works: I'm a native son and I don't remember Larraburu at all. Neither does my mother — and she was *here* for its heyday. Her San Francisco bread map had Boudin at the center and Colombo on it; Parisian and Larraburu simply weren't her brands. And yet in one of those only-in-San-Francisco twists, she at one point lived directly across the street from the Cliff House — where Parisian sourdough was on every table. The city's great breads were so woven into daily life that you could live across the street from one and swear loyalty to another without ever thinking of them as rivals. It was all just *the bread*.

What I do remember is seeing Parisian and Colombo wrappers everywhere — the markets, even the airport. I couldn't have told you my brand. What I could have told you, even then, was that I was walking past the mass-market wonder-dough every single time.

And the Larraburu starter? That's the part that keeps bread people up at night. A mother culture fed continuously since the 1800s doesn't just stop existing — somebody scoops a bit into a jar, a former employee keeps it going in a garage, a freezer somewhere holds a gray-brown lump of history. The hunt for the surviving Larraburu mother is a genuine San Francisco mystery, and SFGate ran a wonderful piece chasing the claims. Honestly, even if the missing starter is never found, the search itself has become exactly the kind of story this bread deserves — equal parts history, obsession, and fog.

## The squishy interlude, and the comeback

After World War II, America fell hard for industrial bread — soft, white, sliced, sweet, and shelf-stable, everything sourdough is not. Across most of the country, wild fermentation all but disappeared from commercial baking. San Francisco simply... declined to participate. The old bakeries kept their mothers fed and their fog-cooled fermentation rooms running, and the city stayed weird in exactly the right way.

That stubbornness paid off. When the American artisan bread movement caught fire in the 1980s and '90s, San Francisco was the reference point — the one American city where the old method had never stopped. And when COVID-19 locked the world indoors in 2020, sourdough became the global comfort project, and every one of those millions of new jars of bubbling flour paste was, whether its owner knew it or not, a little tribute act to San Francisco. The city persevered through every era that tried to kill this bread, which is why "San Francisco" and "sourdough" remain welded together in a way no other city and bread quite manage.

## The plot twist: the city that named a microbe

In 1971, two scientists — Frank Sugihara and Leo Kline — went looking for what actually made San Francisco sourdough tick, and they found a previously undescribed bacterium running the show. They named it ***Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis***, after the city. (A quick correction to a common mix-up, my own notes included: it's a *bacterium*, not a yeast. The bacterium is only half of a double act — its partner is a wild yeast, *Kazachstania humilis*, and the two have a deal worthy of a heist movie: the yeast can't eat maltose, the dough's most abundant sugar, so the bacterium takes the maltose and leaves the other sugars for the yeast. Nobody competes, everybody eats, the bread rises, and the bacterium pays the rent in lactic and acetic acid — the tang.)

For decades this fed the legend perfectly: *of course* you can't make real sourdough anywhere else — our bread has its own microbe! It's in the fog! And then researchers went looking… and found *L. sanfranciscensis* thriving in sourdoughs all over the world. Italian panettone. German rye. Backyard jars on other continents. It turns out the city didn't have a monopoly on the organism; it just got there first with a naming opportunity. (Taxonomists have since reshuffled the genus and it's formally *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* now, which I refuse to say out loud, but the species name — the city's name — survived the reshuffle.)

So the myth deflates, but the story gets *better*: what makes San Francisco sourdough San Franciscan isn't a magic local microbe. It's the *process* — and processes travel.

## Chasing the profile in a Georgia kitchen

That distinctive SF tang leans heavily on **acetic acid** — the sharper, vinegar-side note — rather than the milder, yogurt-like lactic acid. The old San Francisco bakeries got there through long, cool fermentation, which the city's fog handled for free. In Adairsville, I have to schedule my fog. Here's how the levers translate, with the deep dives living in the guides:

The biggest lever is **temperature and time**: cool, slow fermentation shifts the balance toward acetic acid, so a long cold retard in the refrigerator (12–24 hours or more) is the single most San Francisco thing you can do to a dough. Warm and fast gives you a milder, lactic-leaning loaf; cool and slow gives you the tang. The whole warm-lactic/cool-acetic mechanism — and why pH is the clock face you're actually reading — is covered in [pH, Flour, Hydration, and Temperature: The Four Levers](/guides/ph-flour-hydration/).

The second lever is your **starter's hydration and maintenance rhythm**: a stiffer, cooler-kept starter drifts acetic, while a wet, warm, frequently-fed one stays mild and lactic. If you want your jar to develop a San Francisco attitude, [Starter Types, Hydrations, and Flours](/guides/starter/starter-types-and-flours/) covers the hydration spectrum, and [Maintaining a Starter](/guides/starter/maintaining-a-starter/) covers the feeding-rhythm side of the same trade.

And third, a nod to **restraint in the flour bill**: the classic SF loaf is a mostly-white, high-protein dough — chewy, tight-crumbed, built to carry crust and tang rather than whole-grain flavor. Push the whole grains and you're baking something wonderful, but you're drifting away from the profile; [Flour Types and Blending](/guides/flour-types-and-blending/) covers that trade-off.

None of this requires a cable car outside your window. The microbe is probably already in your jar; the fog is just a refrigerator with better scenery.

## Last slice

Researching this post brought back more than I expected — the Wharf, the wrappers, many long lunches at Tadich Grill with Parisian sourdough on the table. It also brought a touch of loss. Parisian closed in August 2005 after nearly a century and a half. Colombo's ovens went cold in November 2012. The storied names had already been gathered up into the San Francisco French Bread Company, then passed to one national conglomerate after another until the brands were finally swept up and discarded — though I admit I laughed when I learned the Colombo name was later acquired by, of all things, *Bimbo Bakeries*, where it technically survives to this day. The wonder-dough people ended up owning a piece of the thing they couldn't beat.

But the mother doughs are harder to kill than the companies. Boudin's bucket is still being fed after 175 years. Larraburu's may yet be sulking in someone's refrigerator. Mine lives in a jar in Adairsville, Georgia, three thousand miles from the fog, raising bread for a native son who still measures every loaf against a slice eaten at Fisherman's Wharf next to a San Francisco girl at heart — past ninety now, and never once wrong about which sourdough is the best.
