My mother's people have opinions about bread they didn't make, and lately those opinions have been about salt rising bread. Someone brought it up at a family gathering — the Dutch side, predictably, since they've never met a strong opinion they didn't want to have first — and now I'm getting texts asking when it's going up on the site. So let's actually look at it, because the honest answer is more interesting than a simple yes or no.

Here's the short version: salt rising bread isn't sourdough. Not "different sourdough." Not "sourdough's cousin." It runs on an entirely different fermentation, and understanding why is the whole reason this bread is worth writing about here — even though I don't think it belongs on the recipe pages.

**What it actually is.** Salt rising bread is an Appalachian tradition, first recorded in a 1778 recipe from what's now West Virginia, back when it was still the western edge of Virginia. It spread from there through the mountains, the coal towns, and eventually up into Pennsylvania and New York — and it shows up in Malinda Russell's 1866 cookbook, the first cookbook published by an African American woman, which tells you something about how far and how ordinary this bread once was. None of that traces back to the Netherlands. The earliest hands on it were mountain settlers making do, not anyone crossing the Atlantic from Amsterdam.

**Why it's not sourdough.** A sourdough starter runs on wild yeast and lactobacilli — the same organisms, more or less, doing the same job, whether the jar is in my kitchen or a bakery in San Francisco. Salt rising bread runs on bacteria, full stop, no yeast involved, and it does it in stages: Bacillus species get things going first, then Clostridium perfringens — yes, that Clostridium, the same genus behind food poisoning in other contexts, present here in a strain and process that's been safely eaten for two centuries — takes over as the real engine of the rise, and Lactobacillus shows up at the tail end. It ferments faster and hotter than a sourdough starter, six to sixteen hours around 104°F instead of a sourdough's cooler, slower schedule, and it announces itself while it works: the starter is famously funky, often compared to ripe cheese or worse, which is exactly why the tradition nearly died out once the commercial starter mix that helped bakeries manage the smell went off the market in the 80s and 90s. What survived, survived in kitchens, passed down instead of written down — which is also why it's gotten hard to find.

**About that "Dutch" claim.** Here's a wrinkle worth clearing up before it goes any further — and I'm glad it got cleared up, because the real story is a lot better than the one I almost let stand. Salt rising bread's history runs through Appalachia, largely by way of English and Scots-Irish settlers, and the "Dutch" that sometimes gets attached to that world — "Pennsylvania Dutch" — is generally understood to be an English mangling of *Deutsch*, German, not a Netherlandish word at all. So if that's the "Dutch" doing the demanding at your family gathering, it's a case of mistaken identity, and salt rising bread was never going to be the vehicle for it either way.

My family's actual Dutch side didn't stop anywhere near Pennsylvania. It made a full circuit of the globe — Netherlands to Southeast Asia, through Malaysia and Singapore, and eventually across the Pacific to the West Coast of the U.S., where it landed for good. That's not a borrowed label or a Wikipedia coincidence; that's a real, multi-generation migration with an actual story in it, and it deserves its own post rather than a paragraph riding along on someone else's bread. Consider this the marker I'm planting for it — Netherlands to the Straits to San Francisco is a much better use of everyone's attention than salt rising bread ever was going to be.

**So does it earn a spot on the site?** Running it through the same six questions I use for everything else here: it's not a family recipe of ours, it isn't a base other recipes can build on, it doesn't hook to my own story the way Larraburu or the San Francisco post do, and — now that the family history's actually on the table — the real ancestral thread runs through the Netherlands and Southeast Asia, nowhere near this bread. What it does have is geography — Adairsville sits right in the Appalachian foothills, which puts salt rising bread's home territory closer to my front porch than San Francisco sourdough ever was — and it illustrates something genuinely useful: what sourdough is by showing, in detail, what it isn't. That's not nothing. That's actually the best case a bread can make for itself when it isn't going to become a recipe here.

Which is the actual verdict: no recipe card, no baker's percentages, no spot next to Well Bread and Sunshine Brioche. But a blog post, written once, explaining honestly what this bread is and isn't — that earns its keep. Consider the family appeased, and consider notice served that the real Dutch story is coming next.
